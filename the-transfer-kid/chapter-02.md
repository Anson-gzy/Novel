# Chapter 2: Group Work

I checked my schedule that night.

Not because I was obsessing over it. I just wanted to know how many classes I'd have to deal with Andrew in, so I could mentally prepare for however much awkward babysitting was headed my way.

I pulled up the class list on my phone and compared it to what Tyler had said about Andrew's schedule — all AP, everything. I started going through mine. English Lit — standard. He'd be in AP English. Biology — standard. He'd be in AP Bio. Math — definitely standard. No way we overlapped there.

One class. We shared one class: World History, third period, Tuesdays and Thursdays.

That was it. Everything else he was taking existed in a world I would never set foot in. AP Chemistry? I could barely handle regular Chemistry, and even that was generous — I'd gotten a D on the last lab report because I forgot to label the axes on my graph. AP Literature? I literally used SparkNotes for the assigned reading last week and still felt proud of my B-minus.

One class out of seven. I could handle that.

I felt something close to relief, which immediately made me feel stupid. It wasn't like sharing more classes with him would've been some kind of punishment. He hadn't done anything wrong. He was just a person. A person who walked through hallways like a ghost who didn't know he was dead and made me feel weirdly nervous for no reason I could explain.

Whatever. One class. Fine.

---

Tuesday came, and I walked into World History exactly on time for maybe the first time all semester. Not on purpose — I just didn't get stuck behind anyone in the hallway.

The room was half full. Mr. Reeves was standing at the front with that dangerous energy teachers get when they're about to announce something nobody wants to hear.

"Group assignment," he said, before I'd even sat down. "Pairs. Due next Thursday. Pick a topic from the list on the board, come up and sign your names."

Low groans from around the room. Someone in the back said "come on" under their breath. The usual.

I dropped my bag next to my desk and did the automatic scan — checking who was where, who I knew, who I could painlessly pair with. Dylan wasn't in this class. Tyler wasn't either. The people I did recognize were already making eye contact with each other, forming pairs with the efficient speed of people who had done this before and knew the drill.

Then I saw Andrew.

He was sitting in the back corner, two rows behind where I usually sat, notebook already open, pen resting across the page. He was reading the topic list on the board. Not looking around the room for a partner. Not seeming bothered that everyone else was already pairing off. Just reading the board, like this was a task he was going to handle alone and that was perfectly fine.

And I thought about Principal Nakamura. His patient face. His waiting-you-out silence. *Make an effort.*

I really didn't want to.

But the thing about me is, even when I don't want to do something, if I know I'm supposed to, it eats at me. Not right away. Just enough to be annoying. Like a notification sitting on your phone that you haven't opened and can't stop seeing.

So I grabbed my bag and walked over.

"Hey," I said.

Andrew looked up. His eyes were dark — not in a dramatic way, just dark brown, almost black in the classroom lighting. He looked at me the way you'd look at someone who just tapped your shoulder in a store. Polite acknowledgment. Zero investment.

"I'm Justin," I said. "We haven't officially met. But I was thinking we could pair up. For the assignment."

He didn't say anything right away. For about three seconds I stood there next to his desk, backpack hanging off one shoulder, wondering if he'd heard me, if he was going to say no, or if this was some kind of power thing where he just let people hang until they walked away.

Then he said, "Sure."

One word. No smile. No "thanks for asking." No "I'm Andrew," even though that's the kind of thing you just do when someone introduces themselves. Just: sure. Like I'd asked him if this seat was taken on a bus.

I sat down at the empty desk next to him. The chair made a loud scraping sound on the floor that I immediately wished it hadn't.

"So," I said. "Any topic you're interested in?"

He glanced at the board again. "The Cold War's straightforward."

"Works for me."

Silence. Not the comfortable kind that happens when two people are chill with each other. The other kind. The kind where you realize neither of you is going to fill the space, and you just sit there hearing other pairs talk around you — laughing, arguing over topics, pushing desks together — while your desk feels like a quiet island in the middle of a noisy ocean.

I pulled out my notebook, which was mostly doodles and half-finished homework, and flipped to an empty page. "Do you want to split it up, or work on it together, or—"

"I can handle the research," Andrew said. Same flat tone. Same neutral face. "You can do the presentation slides if you want."

"Yeah. Okay."

And that was the conversation. All of it. The entire exchange.

We worked for the rest of the period. Or — more accurately — he worked, and I orbited around whatever he was doing. He had his laptop open within thirty seconds and started pulling up sources, reading them at a speed that made me feel like I'd been approaching reading wrong my entire life. His notes were organized. His handwriting was clean and even, the kind that could've come from a font pack. He highlighted things in his textbook with precision — not whole sentences, just the three or four words that mattered.

I opened Google Slides. I picked a color scheme. Blue and white, because it felt serious enough. I typed "The Cold War" on the title page and then sat there trying to think of what else to do with myself.

At some point I read a paragraph about the Berlin Blockade and got confused. "Wait," I said. "Is this the same thing as the Berlin Wall?"

"No." Andrew didn't look up from his screen. "The blockade was 1948. The wall was 1961."

Not mean about it. Not showing off. Just a fact delivered at room temperature. Or below room temperature. Whatever temperature answers come at when someone is giving you information the way a search engine would — accurate, immediate, empty.

"Right," I said. "Got it."

The thing is, I kept trying. Not trying hard, but trying in the way I normally do — throwing out small, casual things to see what sticks. I mentioned something about the cafeteria food being bad. Nothing. I made a joke about Mr. Reeves looking like he needed a vacation. Nothing. I said "this is a lot of reading" in that tone that's supposed to invite agreement or commiseration, and Andrew just said, "It's standard material," and kept typing.

Every opening I tried, he closed. Not aggressively. Not like he was shutting me down or telling me to leave him alone. More like the openings simply didn't exist from his perspective. I was talking. He was hearing me. And then he was continuing with what he'd been doing, untouched by whatever I'd said, the way water moves around a stone.

The bell rang.

Andrew closed his laptop and slid it into his bag in one smooth motion. He stood up like he'd been counting down the minutes.

"I'll send you the research notes tonight," he said. "You can build the slides from there."

I blinked. "Wait — don't we need to meet up after school to finish this? It's due Thursday."

He pulled his bag over his shoulder. "No."

"No?"

"The research is done. You have the structure. Just put it together."

I stared at him. "Dude. I made a title slide. That's literally all I did in the last forty-five minutes. You watched me do it."

He didn't react to that. Not embarrassment for me, not frustration, nothing.

"I'm a C-plus student," I said, and I don't know why I was arguing — normally I would've been happy to get out of extra work. But something about the way he was handing me a whole presentation on the Cold War and walking away felt wrong. Not unfair-wrong. Just strange. "On a good day. My last lab report got a D because I forgot to label the axes on a graph. You really want me to put together the whole thing by myself?"

Andrew looked at me.

And for the first time all period — maybe the first time since I'd met him — it didn't feel like he was looking through me. His eyes settled on mine, and something in his face shifted. Not a smile. Not warmth. Nothing I could point to and call an expression. But the blankness cracked, just barely, just enough for me to notice something behind it. A pause. A beat of something that wasn't nothing.

"You'll be fine," he said. Then, quieter, like it was a fact he'd already decided and wasn't interested in defending: "I trust you."

He left. Same pace as always. Bag over one shoulder, out the door, gone. No goodbye, no "see you Thursday," no looking back.

I sat there.

*I trust you.*

Three words. From a guy who had given me maybe fifteen words total in the last forty-five minutes. A guy who hadn't smiled at me once, hadn't asked me a single question about myself, hadn't shown any sign that he saw me as anything other than someone who happened to sit at the desk next to his.

*I trust you.*

I stared at the title slide on my screen — "The Cold War" in blue Helvetica on a white background — and tried to make it make sense.

It didn't.

---

Dylan was leaning against the water fountain outside. "How's your boy?"

"He's not my boy."

"How's your ward? Your charge? Your assigned companion?"

"He's... weird."

Dylan raised an eyebrow. "Weird how?"

"He did all the work in thirty minutes, told me to put together the entire presentation by myself, and then said he trusts me." I heard myself say it and it sounded even stranger out loud. "Me. The guy who made a title slide and asked if the Berlin Blockade was the same thing as the Berlin Wall."

Dylan stared at me for a second. Then he laughed. "Maybe he doesn't know your GPA."

"He literally watched me contribute nothing for a whole class period. He knows."

"So maybe he just doesn't care."

"That's the part that's weird."

Dylan looked at me for a beat longer than normal. "You're thinking about this a lot."

"I'm not thinking about it a lot."

"You just described a forty-five-minute class period to me in real time. With quotes. That's a lot."

"Shut up."

But walking to my next class, I couldn't stop turning it over. Not the assignment — I'd figure that out, or I wouldn't, and either way it was just a presentation. It was the way he'd said it. *I trust you.* Like he'd looked at me — really looked, for the first time — and decided something. Not based on my grades, not based on anything I'd actually shown him. Just decided.

Everyone else who'd tried to interact with Andrew that week had gotten the same thing: flat responses, zero eye contact, a wall built out of calm. He'd given the football guys nothing. He'd given the people who tried to talk to him in the hallway nothing. He'd given me nothing for forty-five straight minutes.

And then, right at the end, three words that felt like a door cracking open an inch.

Why?

I didn't know. And the not knowing was the part that got me. The silence from before — that, I could ignore. I could file it under "weird kid" and move on with my day. But this was different. This was a question I hadn't asked for, sitting in my head with no answer attached to it, and no obvious reason to care.

But I cared. A little. Enough to notice that I cared.

I went to my next class and opened Google Slides on my phone, scrolling through Andrew's research notes that had already landed in my inbox. Organized. Thorough. With a small note at the top that said: *Use whatever structure you want.*

I read it twice, then put my phone away.

Whatever structure I want. He trusts me.

Who says that to someone they've known for one class period?
