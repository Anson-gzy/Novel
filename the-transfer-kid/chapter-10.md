# Chapter 10: Deterioration

*(POV: Marcus)*
The first thing I noticed was the silence.

Not silence between me and Andrew — that was old news, that was the wallpaper of my life now. I mean the silence between Justin and Andrew. The specific, loaded absence of a person in Justin's sentences where that person used to live.

It started in mid-November, maybe a little earlier. Hard to pin down because it wasn't an event — it was a subtraction. Justin stopped saying Andrew's name. Not dramatically, not with an announcement. He just stopped. The way you stop mentioning a restaurant that closed or a show that got cancelled. One week Andrew was a topic that came up at least twice a day — at lunch, in the group chat, in the small moments between classes — and the next week he wasn't.

The group didn't push it. That was the first thing I found interesting. Dylan, who could normally extract a confession from a locked safe if he was curious enough, didn't ask. Samira, who noticed everything and commented on half of it, said nothing. Even Tyler, who had the social filter of a broken faucet, seemed to sense that this particular silence had a "do not disturb" sign hanging on it.

So I watched. It's what I do.

---

Justin was fine on the surface. He showed up to lunch. He made jokes. He participated in conversations with the right amount of energy at the right moments. He was, by any external measurement, the same Justin he'd been before — slightly lazy, comfortably sarcastic, present in the way that made you forget he was dealing with anything underneath.

But I know Justin. I've known him since freshman year, and I know the difference between his real self and his coping self. His real self is loose. His real self interrupts people and laughs too loud and says things that are funnier than he realizes. His coping self is quieter. More measured. He still performs — he's too socially competent not to — but the timing is off by a fraction. Like a song played at ninety-five percent speed. You can't hear the difference unless you know the original.

I heard it.

It was in the small things. The way he checked his phone less often, like he'd stopped expecting messages from someone specific. The way he sat at lunch with his tray in front of him and ate mechanically, chewing without registering taste, his eyes on the middle distance. The way he laughed at Dylan's jokes but not with the full exhale — more like a reflex than a response.

Something had happened between him and Andrew. I didn't know what, and I didn't ask, because asking Justin a direct question about his feelings is like trying to open a door by pushing the hinge side. You can do it, technically. But it takes more force than it should and you damage something in the process.

---

I saw it from the other side, too.

In Government, Andrew was the same as always — or the same as his Government-class version of always. Quiet, competent, minimal. But there was something different in the quality of his quiet now. Before, he'd been seventy-percent Andrew, the version that functioned without performing. Now he felt like he'd dropped to sixty. Maybe lower. He still answered when Okoro called on him. He still took notes. But the trailed-off sentences — the ones that used to leak something private before he caught himself — those had stopped completely.

He wasn't editing himself anymore. He just wasn't saying anything worth editing.

I watched him between classes one afternoon. He was walking from Government to wherever he went next, and a girl I didn't know — sophomore, maybe — stopped him to ask something. He answered. Short, functional, polite. Then he kept walking. No lingering. No charm. No magnetism. Just movement from point A to point B, the same way you'd move through a room with no one in it.

The hallway version of Andrew — the hundred-percent version, the one I'd seen with Mia, the one who bent the crowd's trajectory just by standing still — that person was gone. Or hibernating. Or switched off. Whatever Andrew did with his public self when he didn't need it, he'd done it.

Something had happened to him, too. And the timing was too close to Justin's silence to be coincidence.

---

Here's my private read on it, the one I kept to myself:

Andrew showed Justin his real self. Not the classroom version, not the hallway version — the real one. The one underneath all the calibration and the social engineering and the careful management of who got to see what. He let Justin in, and whatever Justin found there was different from what he'd expected. Different in a way that couldn't be fixed by adjusting expectations.

This was consistent with everything I'd already observed. Andrew's warmth was temporary. Situational. Maybe even strategic — a tool he used to draw people in before the distance reasserted itself. I'd experienced it firsthand: the functional conversation in Government, the absolute blankness in the hallway. The cycle of engagement and withdrawal, operated at his discretion, on his schedule.

Justin had been getting the deluxe version of this cycle. Longer engagement period, more warmth, more access. But the withdrawal was always coming. It was baked into the architecture of who Andrew was — someone who let people see a crack and then sealed it before they could get a good look.

I felt a version of vindicated. The thing I'd been sensing for weeks — that Andrew's warmth had limits, that it came with conditions I couldn't see — had been confirmed. And I felt a version of sorry for Justin, because Justin wasn't the kind of person who went into things expecting them to fail. He went in open. He went in honest, even when he was pretending not to. And he'd been honest with Andrew, and Andrew had apparently responded by doing what Andrew always did.

Closing the door.

---

The problem I didn't tell anyone — the thing that sat in my chest like a stone I couldn't cough up — was this:

Somewhere in the last few weeks, I had started to like Andrew.

Not like in the way Dylan would jump on. Not like as a crush word, a label, a thing with a name and a shape. Just — like. An interest that had grown past observation into something less clinical. Something that had a pulse.

It didn't make sense. The person I was describing — the person who looked through me in hallways, who calibrated his warmth like a thermostat, who had apparently hurt my closest friend through some combination of distance and dishonesty — that person was not someone I should be thinking about when I wasn't in Government class.

But I was.

I thought about the trailed-off sentences. The way he'd said "the right to not have your past follow you" and then stopped, like he'd walked to the edge of a cliff he hadn't known was there. The way his voice sounded when he was talking to himself in public — not performing, not seeking a reaction, just processing something out loud that he couldn't keep inside.

I thought about the fact that he'd chosen me to ask for Justin's number. Out of everyone — Dylan, who would have made it a thing; Tyler, who would have told Justin immediately; Becca, who would have asked too many questions — he'd come to me. Because he'd read me correctly. Because he'd understood, in a ninety-second interaction, that I was the person least likely to complicate things.

That kind of perception is rare. And it means something. It means the person sees you — not just your surface, not just your social function, but the machinery underneath. Andrew had seen my machinery and used it, and I respected the precision even as I resented the manipulation.

And somewhere in the space between respect and resentment, something else had started growing. Something quieter and more dangerous than either.

I didn't have a clean explanation for it. It wasn't logical. It wasn't even consistent with my own read of the situation — you don't develop feelings for someone you've identified as emotionally unreliable. That's textbook bad judgment, and I don't do bad judgment.

Except apparently I do, because every time I sat in Government and Andrew was three feet away, not looking at me, not registering me, treating me like a piece of the room — every time that happened, I felt it. A pull. The specific, frustrating gravity of wanting someone to see you who has decided you're not worth seeing.

I kept it completely to myself. Not Dylan, not Samira, not Tyler, not Becca. Definitely not Justin. I buried it in the same place I put things I wasn't ready to deal with, and I went on with my day.

---

The friend group got quieter around the subject of Andrew. Not tense — just quiet. The way a conversation topic dies when it stops producing new information.

Justin didn't mention him. Dylan didn't bring him up. The Instagram group chat still ran at its usual pace — memes, complaints about homework, screenshots of things people found funny — but Andrew's name didn't appear. He'd been a regular feature for weeks: someone had seen him, someone had heard something, someone had a theory. Now he was just a gap in the feed, the conversational equivalent of an empty chair.

Samira noticed. I could tell because she looked at me during lunch one day — not pointedly, not with an expression that demanded anything. Just a look. The kind that said: I see the same thing you see. She held it for about two seconds and then went back to her salad.

Nobody said anything.

We all just moved around the empty space and pretended it had always been there.
