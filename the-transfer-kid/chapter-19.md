# Chapter 19: Marcus

I walked for a while.

Not toward anything. Not away from anything specific. Just — forward. The direction my feet were pointing when I came out of the building, past the doorman who said something I didn't hear, past the parked cars and the streetlights and the small restaurant where we'd sat at a window table two hours ago and talked like everything was fine.

The night was cold. I felt it the way you feel things when you're operating on autopilot — as data, not sensation. Cold. Dark. December. My shoes weren't tied properly. My jacket wasn't zipped. These were facts that existed in the same category as the cracks in the sidewalk and the color of the traffic lights: real, irrelevant, passing through me without stopping.

I took the train.

The Red Line was half-empty, which happens at this hour on a weeknight — just me and a couple of people who looked like they were coming home from shifts, leaning against the windows with the specific exhaustion of people who worked jobs that didn't end at five. I sat in a plastic seat and stared at my reflection in the dark glass across the aisle. The reflection stared back. It looked like someone I knew but didn't particularly want to talk to.

The sequence of events replayed without me choosing to replay it.

Andrew's face when I gave him the book. The look that lasted too long. The restaurant with the Thai food and the easy conversation and the way he'd laughed — actually laughed — at something I'd said, and how the sound of it had made something bloom in my chest like a physical thing. His hand on my arm. My hand on his face. The ceiling. The calm. The specific, extraordinary calm of lying next to someone you've wanted to be close to for months and finally being close, and the world outside the room going quiet, and everything feeling, for the first time since September, like it was exactly where it was supposed to be.

And then: *I have a partner.*

Three words. Four syllables. The whole architecture of the evening collapsing inward like a controlled demolition — every beam and wall and carefully placed brick falling in sequence, each one taking something with it on the way down.

*Someone from my old school. We've been together since the summer.*

Since the summer. Before me. Before everything.

I sat on the train and let the replay run. It didn't get easier with repetition. It didn't change shape. It just kept playing — the same moments, the same words, the same feeling of the ground disappearing from under me while Andrew lay on his back and looked at the ceiling and said the sentence that turned three months of my life into something I didn't have a category for.

---

I got home. My mother was asleep. The house was dark except for the light above the stove, which she always left on — not for me, she said, for the house. Like the house needed to know someone would be coming back.

I went to my room. I sat on my bed. I didn't turn on the lights.

The sequence of events replayed again. But this time, it wasn't just the evening. It was everything — the whole run, start to finish, three months compressed into a single pass.

The first day. Andrew in the hallway with his posture and his unreadable face and his ability to walk through a crowd without reacting to a single person in it. Principal Nakamura's office. *Look after him.* The group project. *I trust you.*

The bracelet.

I stopped there. The bracelet. Silver. Tiffany. On his left wrist, catching the light when he wrote. *Someone gave it to me. Someone who isn't around anymore.*

Someone who isn't around anymore.

I'd assumed — at the time, in the moment, without much thought — that "someone" meant a family member. His parents. A relative. Someone connected to the loss that had reshaped his life. The bracelet was a remnant. A memory. A thing you wear because taking it off means letting go of the person who gave it to you.

Now I replayed the sentence with different information. *I have a partner. Someone from my old school.*

The bracelet was from the partner. Not a dead parent. Not a memorial. A gift from someone alive, someone Andrew was with, someone he'd been with "since the summer" while simultaneously sitting next to me in World History and saying *I trust you* and showing me his playlist and looking at me in ways that made me change my shirt twice.

The phone. The typing indicator. The face-up screen. The quick responses to someone who wasn't me. The gradual withdrawal — the warmth dialing down by degrees, the attention shifting, the gap that I'd noticed and Marcus had noticed and Samira had noticed and nobody had named.

Marcus.

I sat up straighter in the dark.

Marcus. Who had been asking about Andrew. Who had been watching Andrew. Who had been "weird" for weeks in a way that Dylan had noticed and Samira knew about and nobody would explain. Marcus, who sat in Government class with Andrew four days a week. Marcus, who shared a room with Andrew for an hour every day in a way that none of the rest of us did.

Marcus had access. Marcus had proximity. Marcus had been paying a kind of attention to Andrew that went beyond curiosity — I'd seen it, even if I hadn't named it. The way he tracked Andrew in the hallways. The way he went quiet when Andrew's name came up. The way Samira looked at Marcus sometimes with that gentle, sharp expression that meant she knew something and was choosing not to share it.

Why Marcus?

The question sat in the dark with me. I turned it over. I ran the math again — different this time, with new variables.

Andrew had a partner. Marcus had been paying specific attention to Andrew for weeks. Samira knew something she wouldn't share. Marcus had gone quiet in a way that was different from his usual quiet — not analytical, not observational, but reduced. Like someone who had received information that had taken something out of him.

The same way I felt right now.

I built a picture. Not carefully — not with Marcus's analytical precision or Samira's observational patience. I built it fast, with anger and hurt and three hours of sleeplessness doing the construction work, and the picture I built was this: Marcus and Andrew. Marcus was the partner. The "old school" part was a lie — or a deflection, or a half-truth, the kind of thing Andrew would say to keep the real answer at a distance. Andrew, who controlled every word that came out of his mouth, who designed every interaction, who had told me "I asked around" when I'd asked how he got my number — Andrew would absolutely say "someone from my old school" when he meant "someone who sits across from me in Government class four days a week." He'd say it because the truth was too close, and proximity makes truth dangerous, and Andrew managed danger the way he managed everything: with precision and misdirection.

Marcus had been at West Valley the whole time. Marcus had been watching Andrew for weeks. Marcus had been giving Andrew things — his attention, his silence, his quiet analytical focus — and Andrew had been receiving them, and the whole time I'd been running my own calculations and reaching my own conclusions and lying awake at 3 AM, the answer had been sitting across from me at lunch.

I knew, somewhere underneath the anger, that the math didn't add up perfectly. If Marcus was the partner, why had Andrew said "old school"? Why had Marcus seemed genuinely surprised when Andrew walked into Government? There were pieces that didn't fit, gaps in the logic that a calmer version of me would have noticed and flagged for further analysis.

But I wasn't calm. I was sitting in my room in the dark at 1 AM with my shoes still untied and the taste of Thai food in my mouth and the phantom sensation of Andrew's hand on my arm and the sentence *I have a partner* running on a loop in my head, and the math didn't need to be perfect. It just needed to be coherent. It just needed to point in a direction that gave my feeling a target.

It pointed at Marcus.

---

I didn't sleep. Not really. I lay in bed and closed my eyes and the replay kept running — not just the evening now but the last three months, every interaction with Marcus and Andrew annotated with the new information, recontextualized, reconstructed into a narrative that supported the conclusion I'd already reached.

Marcus asking for secrecy about the phone number. Marcus watching Andrew in the hallways. Marcus going quiet at the table. The way Marcus had been looking at Andrew — not the way you look at a classmate, not the way you look at a friend's situation you're observing from the outside. The way you look at someone who matters to you in a way you can't say out loud.

I'd seen that look. I'd felt it on my own face.

*He stopped mid-sentence three times in two weeks. Just — stopped. Like he hit a wall he didn't know was there.*

Marcus had said that about Andrew, weeks ago, in a conversation I'd been part of. And I'd recognized it — I'd said the same thing to Dylan, or something close to it. That Andrew did stuff that seemed calculated but then occasionally cracked. That the surface he showed the world had fissures in it, and if you watched long enough, things leaked through.

Marcus and I had been watching the same fissures. Tracking the same cracks. Running parallel observations on the same person, and neither of us had known — or admitted — that the observation was fueled by something personal.

The thought made me sick. Not nauseous — the other kind of sick. The kind that sits in your chest and makes your breathing shallow and your jaw tight and your hands curl into shapes they don't normally make.

Marcus knew. Marcus had known. And Marcus had sat across from me at lunch, every day, watching me deteriorate, watching the silence build between me and Andrew, and he hadn't said a word. Because he was protecting what he had. Because Andrew was his, and I was just the kid the principal had assigned, the accidental complication in a relationship that predated my involvement.

By the time the sun came up, the decision had already been made. Not formally — I didn't sit down and plan what I was going to do. The decision just existed, the way anger exists after a certain point: not as a choice but as a state, like weather, like temperature, like something that was always going to happen once the conditions were right.

---

Thursday.

School was a thing I moved through without participating in. Classes happened. Teachers talked. The hallways had their usual traffic patterns, the usual noise, the usual texture of five hundred teenagers navigating the space between childhood and whatever comes after. I was among them but not part of them. I was somewhere else — somewhere inside my own head, where the replay was still running and the math was still pointing at Marcus and the anger was sitting in my chest like a fist.

I went to lunch.

The table was the same. Dylan talking. Becca on her phone. Tyler eating chips. Marcus across from me. Samira next to me. The usual low hum of a group that had been doing this long enough to have its own rhythm.

I sat down and ate my food and said nothing. Or close to nothing — enough to not be conspicuous, enough to pass as present. Fine. I was fine. The word came out of my mouth when it was needed, automatically, the social equivalent of a screensaver — displaying something that looked like function while the actual system was somewhere else entirely.

Marcus was quiet too. I watched him without watching him — the peripheral awareness that I'd been running on everyone for weeks, now focused to a point. He was eating. He was sitting. He was there, the same way he was always there, steady and analytical and contained, and I hated him for the containment. I hated him for the steadiness. I hated him for the ability to sit across from me and be fine while I was falling apart from the inside.

I looked at my tray. I looked at the table. I looked at the cafeteria, scanning the room the way I'd scanned it every day for months — not for anyone specific, I told myself, just looking — and then I saw him.

Andrew.

He was crossing the far side of the cafeteria. Not coming toward our table — moving perpendicular to it, heading toward the exit on the east side. He was carrying a book and walking at his usual pace, the one that made it look like every step was planned in advance, and his face was turned away from me, and the bracelet caught the fluorescent light the way it always did, a small flash of silver on his left wrist.

I stood up.

I don't remember deciding to stand. I just did — chair pushed back, tray abandoned, body already in motion before my brain had time to construct a reason. I was going to go to him. I was going to cross the cafeteria and catch up with him and say — what? I didn't know. I didn't have a plan. I just knew that Andrew was walking away and I needed to not let him walk away, needed to close the distance that had been growing for weeks, needed to say something — anything — that would crack through the surface and reach the real person underneath.

I moved fast. Through the gaps between tables, past people I half-recognized, past conversations that died as I passed because something about the way I was moving was too fast, too directed, too unlike the usual traffic pattern of a teenager going to the trash can or the bathroom.

Andrew turned the corner. Out of the cafeteria. Into the hallway.

I followed. Through the doors, into the corridor, my untied shoes slapping the tile, my breathing faster than it should have been.

He was gone. The hallway was long and had three branches, and Andrew could have taken any of them, and by the time I reached the intersection he was nowhere. Just an empty corridor with the fluorescent lights buzzing and the muffled sound of the cafeteria behind me.

I stood there. My hands were shaking. Not from cold — from everything else. The adrenaline, the sleeplessness, the three months of accumulation that had been building toward a moment of confrontation that I'd just missed.

I turned around and walked back to the cafeteria.

---

Marcus was at the table.

Same seat. Same posture. Same steady, contained, analytical Marcus who watched people without blinking and kept his word because he gave it and didn't perform and didn't pretend and didn't leak.

I looked at him from across the room.

And something shifted. Not in the math — the math was the same, the same wrong equation producing the same wrong answer. What shifted was the pressure. The thing in my chest that had been building since last night, since the elevator, since the train, since the dark of my room at 1 AM — it reached the point where holding it was no longer possible. The architecture of my composure, which had been taking on stress for weeks, for months, buckled.

I walked toward the table. Not fast — slow. Deliberate. Each step registering in my body like a countdown.

The cafeteria noise was loud around me. Trays and conversations and the hum of several hundred people in a room designed for slightly fewer. I moved through it like moving through water — resistance on every side, the current of normal life pushing against the direction I was going.

I stopped ten feet from the table.

"Marcus."

It came out loud. Louder than I meant — or exactly as loud as I meant, I couldn't tell anymore. The word cut through the noise of the cafeteria the way a stone cuts through a window, sharp and wrong and impossible to take back.

The table looked up. Dylan's sentence stopped. Becca's phone went down. Tyler froze. Samira turned.

Marcus turned. Halfway. His shoulders coming around, his face moving from profile to something closer to facing me, his mouth starting to open —

I hit him.

It wasn't clean. My fist connected with the side of his jaw, off-center, the impact running up my arm and into my shoulder and somewhere in the back of my head a part of me that was still functioning registered that I had never hit anyone before and it hurt more than I expected — not the knuckles, but the cost. The cost of crossing a line that you can't uncross, of doing a thing that lives in the world permanently, that can't be taken back or edited or replayed with a different ending.

Marcus went down. The tray went. The sound of it — tray on tile, body on tile, the collective intake of breath from everyone close enough to see — cut through the cafeteria noise and left a silence in its place.

I stood over him.

My hand was still half-closed. Not a full fist — I hadn't committed to it all the way, even in the moment. My chest was moving with deep, deliberate breaths, the kind your body takes when it's done something your brain hasn't caught up to yet.

My eyes were wet. Not crying — just wet. The overflow of something that had nowhere else to go.

Marcus looked up at me from the floor. His face was registering something — not pain, not yet, but the specific shock of someone whose analytical distance has been physically removed. For once, Marcus was not the one watching from above. For once, he was the subject, not the observer.

I didn't say anything. I didn't have anything to say. The sentence I'd been building — the accusation, the confrontation, the thing I was going to say to him that would explain why and demand answers — was gone. Punched out of me at the same time I'd punched him. All that was left was the standing and the breathing and the wet eyes and the silence of a cafeteria that was watching us like we were the only two people in the room.

Samira said something. I didn't hear it. Dylan's chair scraped. I didn't look.

I just stood there, looking down at Marcus, and Marcus looked up at me, and the cafeteria held its breath, and neither of us moved.
