# Chapter 4: Tiffany

Thursday. I got to World History four minutes early, which had never happened before in my life.

I sat down at my desk — the one next to where Andrew usually sat — and pulled out my notebook. I opened it to a page that already had notes on it so it looked like I'd been reviewing something. I hadn't. I was just sitting there, trying not to look like someone who'd rushed to get to class before another person arrived.

The room filled up slowly. Two girls from the front row. A guy named Jake who always wore the same gray hoodie. Mr. Reeves came in carrying a coffee that looked like it had already gone cold.

Then Andrew walked in.

He was later than usual — three, maybe four minutes before the bell, cutting it closer than his normal calculated arrival. He came through the door at his regular pace, bag on one shoulder, but something was different. When he passed my desk, he didn't just walk by. He slowed down, caught my eye, and said:

"Hey."

Not "hey" like a question. Not "hey" like he was surprised to see me. Just — hey. Like we did this. Like this was something that happened between us every Tuesday and Thursday, and today was no different.

I blinked. "Hey."

He sat down. Pulled out his laptop. Opened it.

That was it.

But that was enough for me to spend the next sixty seconds staring at my open notebook without reading a single word. Because Andrew Hayes had said hello to me. Voluntarily. Without being spoken to first, without a group assignment forcing us into proximity, without any obligation at all. He'd walked in, looked at me, and chosen to speak.

I know how that sounds. I know it was just "hey." People say "hey" a thousand times a day and it doesn't mean anything. But from Andrew — the guy who'd given the entire school nothing for two weeks, who'd responded to every attempt at conversation with the warmth of a closed door — from him, "hey" felt like a lot.

I was overthinking it. Obviously.

I focused on my notebook and tried to act normal.

---

That's when I noticed the bracelet.

Andrew was writing something — pen moving across his notebook in that clean, even handwriting — and every time his hand shifted, something caught the fluorescent light. A thin silver chain around his left wrist, just loose enough to move when he moved. Simple. Elegant. The kind of thing you'd expect on someone who put thought into their accessories, not on someone who wore the same black jacket and white shirt every day like a uniform.

It didn't fit. Or rather, it fit the version of Andrew that I didn't have access to — the one behind the blank face and the functional sentences. The bracelet was a detail from some other life, some other version of him, and it had slipped through the armor by accident.

I stared at it for probably too long before I opened my mouth.

"That's a nice bracelet."

Andrew looked down at his wrist like he'd forgotten it was there. For a second I thought he was going to do the usual thing — the nothing-response, the blank acknowledgment, the return to whatever he'd been doing. But he didn't.

"It's silver," he said. Then, almost as an afterthought: "Tiffany."

I processed that. Tiffany. Like, Tiffany & Co. The blue box, the whole thing. "Isn't that expensive?"

He turned his wrist slightly, looking at the chain like he was studying it for the first time. "I don't really know. Someone gave it to me."

"Who?"

The word came out faster than I intended. Too direct. Too curious. I braced for the wall to go back up.

But Andrew just shrugged. Not dismissive — more like the answer genuinely didn't bother him. "Someone who isn't around anymore."

He didn't elaborate. He didn't seem sad about it. He didn't seem anything about it. He just turned his wrist back, picked up his pen, and kept writing.

I sat there with the weight of that sentence — *someone who isn't around anymore* — and didn't know what to do with it. His parents? Someone else? The sentence could've meant ten different things, and Andrew had delivered it with the same tone he'd use to tell you the Berlin Blockade was in 1948.

I wanted to ask more. I didn't. Some questions feel like doors you shouldn't open until you've been invited.

---

Mr. Reeves had us work on the Cold War slides for the rest of class. This was our third session on the project, and my section was — if I'm being honest — not great. Andrew had sent me his research notes days ago, organized and thorough, and I'd made slides out of them. But they were messy slides. The kind where the text doesn't line up and half the bullet points are different font sizes because I kept changing my mind.

About twenty minutes in, Andrew leaned over. Not dramatically — just tilted his chair an inch in my direction and looked at my screen.

"How's it going?"

I could've lied. I almost did. But something about the way he asked — quiet, neutral, not checking up on me but genuinely asking — made me decide not to.

"Honestly? Kind of a mess."

He looked at the slides for a few seconds. I waited for the disappointment. The subtle judgment. The "I knew I should've done this myself" energy.

"The content is right," he said. "The structure's just a little scattered. You could move the timeline stuff to one slide and keep the policy breakdown separate."

That was it. No tone. No correction dressed up as criticism. Just a clear suggestion, delivered at the same flat temperature as everything else he said, except it landed different this time. Warmer, maybe. Or not warmer — just closer.

"Yeah," I said. "That makes sense."

"It'll be fine."

Two words. But the way he said them felt like the "I trust you" from last week. A statement that came from somewhere deeper than the surface, even if the surface was all he showed.

---

We kept working, and something happened that hadn't happened before.

We talked.

Not about anything important. Andrew asked if I'd done the reading for the unit — I hadn't, obviously, and he didn't seem surprised. I asked if Reeves always gave this much work or if it was a new thing, and Andrew said he couldn't compare since this was his first semester here. I said something about the school's WiFi being unreliable and he said "it's not great" which was the most aggressively normal sentence he'd ever spoken to me.

The conversation wasn't deep. It wasn't a breakthrough. It was a Tuesday — no, a Thursday — in a classroom, two people talking about school while they worked on slides about the Cold War. The kind of exchange that happens a thousand times a day in every high school in the country and means nothing.

But I kept waiting for it to stop. I kept waiting for Andrew to close back up, to return to the one-word answers and the blank stare and the wall that I'd spent two weeks watching him build. Every time I said something I braced for the silence that usually followed, the thousand-yard stare at his screen, the moment where I'd remember that talking to Andrew was like talking to a polite, intelligent rock.

It didn't come.

He responded to things. He almost asked me a question about the teacher's comment on the Berlin Wall — something about how Reeves always made the dramatic point at the wrong time — and there was something in his voice that was close to amusement. Not a laugh. Not even a smile. But the shadow of one. The space where a smile used to live.

I noticed that too. I noticed everything.

---

The bell rang.

Andrew closed his laptop and started packing up. I was doing the same, slower than usual, trying to figure out if the last forty-five minutes had actually happened or if I'd invented them.

Then he said it.

"Hey." He was standing up, bag on his shoulder, looking at me in that way he had — calm, still, like he'd already made a decision. "Do you want to come over after school?"

I stopped moving.

"My place," he added, like that clarified something. "We could work on the slides. Or not. Whatever."

I should've paused. I should've said "let me check" or "I'll text you" or any of the normal things a person says when they need thirty seconds to figure out what they're feeling. Instead I said:

"Yeah. Sure."

He nodded. "Cool."

And then he left. Same pace. Same posture. Out the door, around the corner, gone.

I stood there next to my desk with my bag half-zipped, staring at the empty doorway.

Andrew Hayes, who spoke in single sentences and carried himself like a person who'd decided he didn't need other people, had just asked me to come to his house. His actual house. After school. Today.

I finished zipping my bag. I picked it up. I walked out of the classroom.

My hands were sweating.

I didn't know what that meant. I wasn't sure I wanted to know.

But I'd said yes. And I'd said it immediately, without thinking, like the answer had been sitting in my mouth waiting for the question.
