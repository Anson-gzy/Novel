# Chapter 27: The East Gate

*(POV: Andrew)*

The noise of the cafeteria did not cut out all at once. It fractured into uneven pockets of sound, like a tray rattling across linoleum before settling flat.

Andrew stood three paces from the double doors with his hands in the pockets of his wool jacket. Around him, students stood on plastic benches, holding up phones and shouting across the rows. In the center of the clearing between tables four and five, Marcus sat on the floor with his back against a steel table leg. Tyler was on one knee beside him, pressing brown paper napkins against Marcus's left cheekbone. Marcus was not yelling. He was staring at the floor with quiet concentration, his chest rising and falling in slow rhythms.

Ten feet away, Vice Principal Davies and two security guards had Justin by the arms.

Justin did not fight them, but he did not help them either. His shoulders were locked, his chin down against his collar, his backpack hanging from one elbow. As they guided him toward the hallway, his sneakers dragged across the floor wax. He did not look at Marcus. He did not look at the crowd. When his path crossed near the doors, his gaze passed over Andrew without stopping, the way someone looks at a fence post while thinking of something else.

Andrew stayed where he was until the heavy doors swung shut, cutting off the view of the corridor.

The talk around the tables was already turning into distinct stories. A junior near the vending machines told a friend that Marcus had stolen Justin's car keys. Another voice claimed it was about money from a party. Andrew listened to the noise rise, feeling the cold weight behind his temples.

For two months, he had treated West Valley High like a set of controlled variables. He had chosen his seat in Government, tracked the traffic between third and fourth period, and spoken only when silence drew more attention than words. He had thought that keeping his past locked away was a form of protection.

Now, looking at the spilled milk and the napkins on the floor, he could see the flaw. Silence did not keep things empty. It left space for other people to build their own shapes out of the dark.

He turned and walked out the side exit into the quad.

***

The front office followed a predictable schedule for physical fights: separate intake rooms, incident reports on carbon paper, medical checks for swelling, and mandatory phone calls to parents before any student left campus. The sequence took at least forty-five minutes.

Andrew skipped his fifth-period Statistics class. He walked past the gym, crossed the gravel behind the auto shop, and stopped near the east exit.

The east gate was a narrow break in the chain-link fence where the concrete sidewalk met the perimeter road. Students who took the bus cut through the gap between the bike racks and the brick wall of the maintenance shed. It was quiet there. The only sound was the distant traffic on the boulevard and the dry rustle of eucalyptus leaves blowing across the asphalt.

Andrew rested his backpack on the curb and leaned against the brick wall.

The afternoon air was thin and cool. Above the valley, the December sky was a pale, washed-out blue. He touched the outer pocket of his bag, feeling the hard rectangular edge of the book Justin had given him on Friday night. He had spent the weekend in Brentwood with Zack, letting the quiet of the penthouse settle over him, believing he could return on Monday and draw a neat line between his lives. He had planned to return the book, give a brief explanation, and close the ledger.

He looked at his watch. It was 1:22 PM.

At 1:34 PM, the heavy metal door of the office wing opened with a dull clatter.

Justin walked out alone. He wore an oversized gray sweatshirt, the cuffs pulled down over his hands. In his left hand, he held a folded yellow copy of a disciplinary referral slip. His walk lacked its usual loose rhythm. His feet moved forward simply because the ground was in front of him.

Andrew stepped into the open space near the bike racks.

"Justin."

Justin stopped. His back went rigid, but he did not turn immediately. He stood on the edge of the asphalt for three seconds, looking at the street ahead.

Finally, he turned his head halfway. His eyes were bloodshot, ringed with dark circles from days of poor sleep. His right hand was tucked into his sweatshirt pocket, but the swollen shape of his knuckles was visible through the fabric.

"Don't," Justin said. His voice was cracked and thin.

"I need two minutes," Andrew said, keeping his tone low so it did not carry toward the parking lot. "Marcus had nothing to do with it."

Justin turned fully. He did not yell. He just looked at Andrew with an expression that had no curiosity left in it.

"What did you say?" Justin asked.

"Marcus is not the person from my old school," Andrew said. He laid the sentence out carefully, like a step in a geometry proof. "I asked him for your phone number in October because I knew he did not care about school gossip. He never pays attention to group chats and he does not ask questions. I asked him because he was safe. That was all it was."

Justin looked at him. A small twitch appeared at the corner of his mouth, then vanished.

"You asked him," Justin said slowly.

"Yes."

"Because he was safe."

"Yes," Andrew said. "And the partner I told you about, the one from Westbrook, his name is Zack Chen. He lives in Brentwood. He has never been on this campus. Marcus did not know anything about him, and Marcus never knew what happened between us. If you just look at the timeline, you will see why it happened."

"Stop talking," Justin said.

The words were so flat that Andrew went quiet.

Justin took a step closer, holding the yellow paper in a tight fist.

"Do you really think this is about Marcus?" Justin asked. "Do you think the problem is that I got the wrong name on a sheet of paper?"

"You hit him because you thought he was lying to you about Westbrook," Andrew said.

"I hit him because I lost my mind after four days of staring at the ceiling in my room," Justin said, his voice dropping into a harsh whisper. "I hit him because I sat in your living room on Friday night, gave you that book, thought we were actually talking, and then you dropped a bomb on the floor and watched me walk out into the dark."

Justin took a short breath, his jaw tightening.

"You let me spend three weeks wondering why you went cold," Justin continued. "You let Dylan and Samira make jokes at lunch. You watched me walk into history class every Tuesday and Thursday like an idiot, trying to figure out what I did wrong. And the whole time, you had everything measured out in your head. You knew the names. You knew the rules. You just did not think I needed to know them."

"I did not tell you because it was not simple," Andrew said. He felt an unfamiliar hesitation in his chest. "I was trying to keep things from getting complicated before I knew how to handle them."

"No," Justin said. "You were keeping yourself safe. That is all you do. You show up, give people five percent of the truth, and then act surprised when they cannot see the rest of the picture."

Justin pointed the folded yellow paper at Andrew's chest.

"Save the explanation, Andrew. Keep your timeline. I do not care who Zack is. I do not care why you talked to Marcus. I do not care about your old school, or your penthouse, or whatever game you play to feel in control. I am suspended for three days, and when I come back, I am switching history groups to sit with Tyler. We are done."

"Justin," Andrew said, taking half a step forward. "Listen to me."

"Do not come near me," Justin said.

The tone was absolute. Justin looked directly into Andrew's eyes for a second, and in that second, Andrew saw that the opening from September, the easy warmth and the boy who had made slides across a library table, was gone. In its place was a wall, thicker and harder than any armor Andrew had built for himself.

Justin turned, crossed the broken asphalt, and walked through the gap in the chain-link fence.

Andrew stood by the bike racks and watched him go. Justin did not run. He walked down the block toward the bus shelter, his hands in his pockets, his shoulders hunched against the breeze. He reached the corner, waited for the light, and crossed the street without looking back.

***

The quiet returned to the east gate.

Andrew stood alone on the pavement for five minutes. The eucalyptus leaves skittered past his shoes, clicking softly against the fence.

His calculations had produced nothing. He had delivered the accurate facts in the correct order, without exaggeration, and it had fixed nothing. It had only proved Justin's point: that Andrew treated human feelings like equations to be solved after the collapse, rather than debts that had to be paid in real time.

He walked across the asphalt to the student parking lot.

He unlocked his BMW, tossed his backpack onto the passenger seat, and got behind the wheel. The car was quiet and warm from the sun. He sat in the driver's seat with his hands on the wheel, looking through the windshield at the concrete wall of the gymnasium.

On the seat beside him, the corner of the hardcover book poked out from his bag. He pulled it out and set it on his lap. The green cloth cover was clean and smooth. There was no argument left to make, no text message that could undo the last two hours, and no way to rewrite the silence he had kept for three weeks.

He placed the book inside the center console, shut the lid, and started the engine.

***

He did not drive back to the penthouse in Glendale.

He turned south toward the freeway, merging onto the 405 South and climbing into the hills. As the car crested the pass, the dry heat of the valley gave way to the cool, gray haze rolling in from the ocean.

He took the Sunset Boulevard exit, followed the residential roads of Brentwood, and pulled into the gravel lot of the Westside Aquatic Center.

The facility was an open-air athletic complex surrounded by privacy hedges and concrete walls. Andrew parked by the far edge of the lot beneath a row of jacaranda trees. Through the open window, he could hear the rhythmic splashing of water, the chirp of a coach's whistle, and the thud of feet on the diving blocks.

He checked the clock on the dashboard. It was 3:45 PM. Afternoon club practice ended at four.

Andrew turned off the engine and leaned his head back against the headrest. He closed his eyes and listened to the sounds of the pool. His breathing slowly leveled out.

At 4:08 PM, the side gate of the pool facility clicked open.

Swimmers began walking out into the parking lot in small groups, carrying mesh gear bags and damp towels. Zack was near the back. He wore black track pants and a faded Westbrook hoodie, his dark hair wet from the shower. His duffel bag hung from his left shoulder, and he walked with the steady pace of an athlete after two hours in the water.

Zack looked across the lot and spotted the blue BMW right away.

He did not hesitate. He walked between the parked cars, stepped onto the gravel beside Andrew's door, and pulled open the passenger side.

The car filled with the sharp smell of pool chlorine, damp cotton, and cold air.

Zack dropped his duffel bag into the footwell and sat down. He clicked his seatbelt into place and turned his head to look at Andrew.

Zack did not ask why Andrew was in Brentwood on a Monday afternoon. He did not ask about school, or classes, or the weekend they had spent together. He simply looked at Andrew's face, taking in the set of his jaw and the fatigue around his eyes.

"You look rough," Zack said.

Andrew rested his forehead against the top of the steering wheel. The cool leather felt good against his skin. For several seconds, he said nothing, letting the quiet presence of another person fill the car.

"I tried to talk to him," Andrew murmured into the crook of his arm.

Zack was silent for a moment. He reached across the console and rested his palm against the back of Andrew's neck, his fingers warm and rough from the water.

"How was it?" Zack asked.

"It was nothing," Andrew said, his voice low against the wheel. "He told me to stay away from him."

Zack's fingers tightened slightly on Andrew's neck, offering a steady weight that asked for no defense or apology. Zack looked through the windshield at the quiet lot, his expression calm.

"Then leave it," Zack said softly. "You did what you could. Now you let it go."

Andrew lifted his head slowly and looked at Zack.

There were no questions in Zack's eyes. There was no demand for an explanation, no debate about who was right, and no expectation that Andrew had to maintain the untouchable version of himself from West Valley High. Zack had pulled him from under a concrete beam in June; he knew every crack in Andrew's foundation, and he was still sitting in the passenger seat.

"Put the car in reverse," Zack said, giving Andrew's neck a gentle squeeze before leaning back. "I am starving. Let's get tacos."

Andrew looked at him for a long beat. A small breath left his lungs, carrying away the last of the tension.

"Okay," Andrew said.

He put his hand on the shifter, pulled it into reverse, and backed out onto the street.
