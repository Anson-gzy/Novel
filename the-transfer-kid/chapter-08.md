# Chapter 8: Schedule Change

*(POV: Marcus)*
My schedule this semester was built for efficiency.

AP English first period — I like words more than numbers, and my brain works better in the morning. Then History, standard, because I don't love history enough to suffer through the AP version. Free third period on Mondays and Wednesdays, which I use for catching up on whatever I fell behind on, or sitting in the library doing nothing productive and telling myself I'll start in five minutes. AP Government fourth, because I'm considering law school in a way that's more theoretical than practical. Then lunch. Then the afternoon blocks, which change depending on the day and are all fine.

I don't overthink my days. I get up, I go to class, I say what I think — sometimes too fast, sometimes to the wrong person, sometimes both — and I go home. I don't need to be liked by everyone. I need to be honest, mostly, and on time, usually, and done with school before 3:30 because that's when I stop being functional.

That's the thing about me that most people figure out pretty quickly: I don't perform. I don't do the thing where you say something you don't mean to make someone comfortable, or laugh at a joke that isn't funny, or pretend to care about something that doesn't interest you. I just say what's in front of me. Dylan calls this "aggressive honesty." Justin says I have "zero chill." Both of these are accurate.

It works for me. Most of the time.

Tyler's like that too, actually — the only other person in our group who doesn't overthink what he says before he says it. The difference is I don't say things because I don't care whether people like the answer. Tyler doesn't say things because it genuinely doesn't occur to him that the answer might be a problem. We're both honest; I'm honest on purpose and Tyler's honest by accident. I find this extremely entertaining.

---

Andrew Hayes showed up in my fourth-period Government class on a Wednesday.

I'd been in that class for six weeks without him. The seat two rows to my left had been empty since the semester started — one of those phantom seats that happens when someone drops a course or never enrolled properly. Nobody sat in it. Nobody noticed it. It was just a gap in the room that the rest of us had absorbed into the scenery.

Then, on a Wednesday in late October, Andrew walked in and sat down.

He didn't announce himself. Didn't walk up to the teacher — Mr. Okoro — and have the new-student conversation. He just came in about a minute before the bell, put his bag on the floor, and opened his notebook. Like he'd been here all along and the rest of us just hadn't noticed.

Mr. Okoro looked up from his desk, checked something on his computer, and said: "Andrew Hayes?"

"Yeah."

"Schedule correction?"

"Yeah. They had me in AP Econ but I'd already taken it."

"Welcome to Government. You're caught up on the readings?"

"I'm caught up."

And that was it. Okoro nodded and went back to whatever he was doing. Andrew settled into the seat, and class started.

I recognized him immediately. Hard not to — he was the guy Justin had been orbiting for the past month, the one Dylan wouldn't shut up about, the one I'd given a phone number to in a hallway two weeks ago. We hadn't interacted since. He probably didn't even remember which friend I was.

But he glanced over during the first ten minutes — a quick look, like he was scanning the room and checking who he knew — and when his eyes hit me, something registered. A nod. Not friendly, not unfriendly. Just acknowledgment. You're the one who gave me the number. I know who you are.

I nodded back.

And then I started paying attention.

---

Here's what I noticed about Andrew Hayes in a class where Justin wasn't present: he was different.

Not dramatically different. Not a whole different person. But the frequency was off — like he was broadcasting on a channel a few notches lower than the one he used around Justin. The easy confidence, the way he carried himself at school like the hallway was his personal red carpet, the thing Dylan described as "magnetism" — it was still there. But turned down. Running at maybe seventy percent.

In Government, Andrew was quiet. Not the quiet that Justin described — not the loaded, meaningful silence that made you wonder what was behind it. Just regular quiet. The kind of quiet that happened when someone wasn't performing for anybody. He took notes. He answered when Okoro called on him — always correct, always concise, no showing off. He didn't volunteer information. He didn't engage with the people around him.

This wasn't the Andrew that Justin talked about. Justin's version of Andrew had weight. Gravity. He was the guy who said "I trust you" and wore a Tiffany bracelet and lived in a penthouse and did things that seemed ordinary but felt enormous. Justin described Andrew the way you describe weather — something powerful and unpredictable that you watched from a distance and tried to understand.

The Andrew in my Government class was none of that. He was just a kid. A smart kid, clearly, who had already read the material and could have run the class if Okoro had asked him to, but a kid who sat in his seat and wrote in his notebook and didn't seem to need anything from anyone in the room.

I found this interesting. Not fascinating — I don't fascinate easily. But interesting.

---

Over the next few weeks, I noticed patterns.

Andrew showed up on time. He did his work. He occasionally made a comment that seemed like it was about the topic — Constitutional checks, legislative process, whatever Okoro was covering that day — but that trailed off into something else. Like he'd started a sentence about the separation of powers and ended up somewhere private, somewhere the sentence was actually about something else, and he'd caught himself and stopped before anyone noticed.

Except I noticed.

It happened three times in the first two weeks. The first time, Okoro asked about judicial review, and Andrew said something about oversight versus trust, and then stopped mid-sentence and said "anyway, the answer is Marbury v. Madison." The second time, someone asked about voter suppression and Andrew started to say something about who gets to decide what people deserve, and then went quiet and stared at his notebook for ten seconds before saying "pass."

The third time, we were doing a mock debate — government transparency versus individual privacy. Andrew was on the privacy side. He made a point about how the right to reinvent yourself depends on the right to not have your past follow you, and it was the longest thing I'd heard him say in class. Then he paused, looked at his hands, and said, "But that's aspirational. Not realistic."

Nobody else noticed. They were all half-asleep or arguing with each other or looking at their phones under the desk. But I was watching, and what I saw was a person who kept walking up to the edge of something real and then pulling back.

It was mildly annoying.

I don't mean that as an insult. I just prefer it when people say what they mean. If you have a thought, say the thought. If you have a feeling, either say the feeling or don't bring it up. The worst version of communication is the kind where someone opens a door, lets you see a crack of something, and then closes it before you can figure out what you saw. It's like someone starting a sentence with "never mind" — if you weren't going to finish, why did you start?

Andrew did this constantly. Not in a manipulative way — I don't think he was trying to be mysterious or interesting. I think he just didn't know when to stop editing himself. Like he'd been doing it so long that the filter was stuck, and sometimes things leaked through before he could catch them.

---

I brought it up with Dylan and Tyler on a Thursday after school. We were walking to the parking lot — or rather, Dylan was walking to his mom's car and Tyler and I were walking in the same direction because our bus stops were that way.

"That guy Andrew," I said. "He's in my Government class now."

"Oh yeah?" Dylan looked at me. "Since when?"

"Couple weeks. Schedule correction."

"Is he weird?" 

"He's weird."

Dylan grinned. "Weird how? Justin-weird or like, actually weird?"

"What's the difference?"

"Justin-weird is when Justin describes something normal that Andrew did and makes it sound like the guy performed a miracle. Like, 'he said hey to me and I nearly died.' That's Justin-weird. I'm asking if Andrew is actually weird."

"He's actually weird. He says things in class that start out being about government and end up being about something else. Like he's having two conversations at once and only one of them is out loud."

"So he's doing the thing where he's being deep but doesn't want anyone to notice," Tyler said.

Dylan and I both looked at him.

"What?" Tyler said. "That's what it sounds like. Like when someone posts a song lyric as their caption and pretends it's about nothing."

That was — actually a pretty good comparison. I wouldn't have put it that way, but Tyler had a talent for accidentally saying the right thing in the wrong words.

Dylan considered this. "Huh."

"He stopped mid-sentence three times in two weeks," I said. "Just — stopped. Like he hit a wall he didn't know was there."

"Maybe he's just thinking out loud," Dylan said.

"Maybe. But it doesn't feel like thinking out loud. It feels like he's saying something he doesn't want to be saying, and he catches himself too late."

"That tracks," Dylan said. "Justin said the same thing, kind of. That Andrew does stuff that seems calculated but then occasionally cracks."

"It's more than occasionally."

Dylan looked at me sideways. "You're paying a lot of attention to this guy."

"He sits two rows away from me four days a week. It's not attention. It's proximity."

"Sure." Dylan didn't push it, which was unusual for him. He just nodded, like he was adding this to a file he'd been building in his head.

"I mean, you literally gave him Justin's number," Tyler said. "You're already more involved than you think you are."

I didn't have a great response to that, so I ignored it.

Samira wasn't with us, but if she had been, she probably would've just nodded. Like she already knew.

---

Friday. Between fourth and fifth period. The hallway near the gym.

I was heading to my locker when I saw Andrew standing near the water fountain, talking to someone. A girl from AP Bio — Mia something, short hair, always carrying a textbook she was never actually reading. She was laughing at something he'd said, and Andrew was standing there with his hands in his jacket pockets, looking at her with the full version of himself turned on.

Not the seventy-percent version I got in Government. The hundred-percent version. The one with the easy posture and the eye contact and the slight tilt of his head that made it look like whatever you were saying was the most interesting thing he'd heard all day. The magnetism was back. The hallway around him had that subtle pull — people glancing as they walked past, trajectories curving slightly like he was bending the current of the crowd just by standing there.

Mia laughed again. Andrew said something I couldn't hear, and she pushed his arm the way people do when someone says something unexpected. He didn't flinch. He just shifted his weight, said something else, and she laughed again.

I stopped at my locker, which was close enough to have a clear line of sight, and watched for maybe ten seconds.

This was the Andrew that Justin described. The one who walked through the school like he owned it. The public version — charming, magnetic, effortlessly at ease. The version that made people turn their heads and teachers pay attention and friend groups spend entire lunch periods dissecting his behavior.

And then there was the Andrew I'd been sitting next to in Government. The one who trailed off mid-sentence. The one who stared at his hands after making a point about reinvention. The one who had asked me for a phone number in a hallway and requested — not demanded, requested — that I keep it between us.

Two versions. Same person.

I closed my locker and walked to my next class.

I didn't know what to do with this. It wasn't a problem I needed to solve — Andrew wasn't my friend, wasn't my responsibility, wasn't someone I'd chosen to pay attention to. He was just a person who'd ended up in my class and happened to be connected to one of my closest friends by something that neither of them seemed fully in control of.

But the thing that stuck with me — the thing I kept coming back to as I walked down the hallway and let the noise of the school wash over me — was that Andrew Hayes wasn't one person. He was at least two, maybe more. And the version he showed you depended on who was watching.

I didn't know which version Justin got. Probably a third one — something between the public charm and the private cracks. Something that was, itself, a kind of performance, even if neither of them knew it.

It wasn't my problem. That's what I told myself. And I meant it.

But I kept watching anyway. Not because I cared. Just because I was there, and he was there, and you don't look away from something you don't understand just because it isn't your business.

You look until you do.
