# Chapter 21: Read

*(POV: Zack)*

The second message came before I could figure out what to say to the first one.

*waderichardson: wait. you actually watched the video. the IKEA one. that's three months old. you scrolled deep.*

I stared at the screen. He was right. I had scrolled deep. That was exactly the kind of thing a normal person would have hidden, and exactly the kind of thing Wade had zeroed in on inside of ten seconds.

I typed back.

*justin.k: the algorithm served it to me. blame the swedes.*

Three dots. Then:

*waderichardson: the swedes get blamed for enough. they gave us meatballs and flat-pack anxiety. leave them alone.*

*waderichardson: so. justin. what do you do when you're not critiquing my carpentry skills?*

I leaned back against my headboard. My room was fully dark now. I hadn't turned on the light.

*justin.k: school. nothing interesting.*

*waderichardson: school where?*

I paused. This was the first decision point. The first place where the lie had to be specific enough to hold weight.

Andrew had talked about Justin. Not a lot, but enough. Public school in the Valley. Normal neighborhood, normal family. The kind of background that Andrew had described the way you describe a place you've never been but find interesting — like a foreign country that operates on simpler rules.

*justin.k: west valley. public school. senior year.*

*waderichardson: west valley. very civilian. very normal-person energy.*

*justin.k: is that a problem?*

*waderichardson: the opposite. I'm surrounded by people who think normal is an insult. it's refreshing.*

The conversation kept going. I didn't plan for it to. I'd expected him to lose interest after three or four messages — the way most people do when they're talking to a blank profile with no content and no mutual connections. But Wade didn't lose interest. He kept asking questions, and the questions were good. Not the kind that feel like an interview. The kind that feel like someone picking locks — gentle, specific, testing which ones open easily and which ones resist.

He asked what music I listened to. I gave him stuff I actually liked — SZA, Frank Ocean, some Japanese city pop playlist I'd found during recovery. That part wasn't a lie.

He asked what I did for fun. I said I swam. Also not a lie.

He asked if I was any good. I said "decent." That was a lie. I was very good.

"Decent swimmers don't say decent," he typed. "They say 'I'm okay' or 'it's just a hobby.' Decent is what good people say when they're being humble. How good?"

I smiled at the screen. Damn. He was sharp.

*justin.k: good enough that it matters. not good enough to go pro.*

*waderichardson: that's the worst zone. caring about something you can't make a career out of. I know that zone.*

*justin.k: you have 1.2 million followers. pretty sure you made a career out of it.*

*waderichardson: I made a living out of it. different thing. a career is something you chose. a living is something that happened to you while you were doing something else.*

I read that twice. It was a good line. The kind of thing that sounds casual but has weight underneath it, and the weight is the real point.

---

He sent the first voice note at eleven.

"Okay," his voice said. It was different hearing it outside a video — less performed, rougher at the edges, like he'd been talking for a while and his voice had worn down to its actual texture. "I'm going to tell you something and you can judge me or not. I don't care."

A pause. I could hear ambient noise — a window open somewhere, distant traffic, the kind of background sound that expensive neighborhoods have at night. Not silence. Just quiet money.

"Making content is boring. I know that sounds — I know. A million people would kill for what I have. But the actual process — filming, editing, posting, checking numbers, responding to comments, doing it again tomorrow — it's the same thing over and over. It's factory work with better lighting."

Another pause. Then: "My mom thinks I should go to college. She's Chinese. Obviously she thinks that. She has a spreadsheet of schools ranked by return on investment. She texts it to me quarterly. Updated. With new data."

I laughed. Out loud. In my dark room, alone, at something a stranger said into his phone.

I recorded a voice note back. I hadn't planned to — typing was safer, more controlled, easier to maintain a character. But his voice was right there in my ear, real and unfiltered, and typing felt wrong. Like answering a handshake with a wave.

"My mom does the same thing," I said. "Except it's not a spreadsheet. It's just a look. She has this specific look that means 'I expected more from you' without saying a single word. Chinese moms communicate in frequencies that aren't technically audible."

He sent a voice note back in under thirty seconds. "FREQUENCIES. Yes. My mom operates on a bandwidth that dogs can hear. She doesn't need words. She just adjusts her posture and you know exactly how disappointed she is on a scale of one to ten."

We went back and forth. Voice notes getting longer. His voice settling into something more relaxed, more real, each recording stripping away another layer of the public version until I was hearing someone who sounded like a person and not a personality.

He talked about his dad — white, from Oregon, "the kind of guy who thinks putting soy sauce on rice is ethnic cooking." He talked about being half — the specific experience of looking ambiguous enough that people always asked, and the exhaustion of the question being the first thing anyone said to you. I knew that experience. I'd lived inside it my entire life. I didn't say that, because saying it as Justin felt wrong — like borrowing someone else's identity to validate my own, which was exactly what I was doing, but thinking about it too directly made the whole thing start to feel unstable.

So I didn't think about it.

He mentioned Moon once. Casually, the way you mention weather.

"Moon's not traditional," he said. The word sat there for a second. "About us. About how things work. He's got his own philosophy about relationships and I've stopped trying to explain it to people because nobody gets it and honestly I'm not sure I get it either. But it works. Whatever it is."

I didn't push. He didn't offer more.

At two in the morning he sent a voice note that was just laughter. Thirty seconds of him losing it over something I'd said about competitive swimming culture — how the whole sport was just organized suffering with a stopwatch — and the laughter was so genuine, so unselfconscious, that I played it twice.

At three-thirty he sent a photo. Just his hand holding a mug of tea, the kitchen dark behind him, one light on over the stove. No caption. Just the image. I looked at it for a while — his fingers, the mug, the specific quality of late-night kitchen light — and felt something shift in my chest. Not the heavy thing from earlier. Something lighter. Warmer. The way a muscle loosens after being held tight for too long.

At four he said, "I should probably sleep. This is the longest DM conversation I've had since Moon figured out how to text."

I said, "Same."

"Tomorrow?" he said.

"Yeah."

"Cool. I'll text you. Give me your number."

I hesitated for exactly one second. Then I gave him my real number. There was no version of this where a fake phone number made sense, and my real number didn't have my name attached to it in any way Wade could trace.

"Night, Justin," he said.

"Night."

---

The next day he texted at noon. iMessage. Green bubble — he was on Android, or I was on a different carrier, or something. It didn't matter.

*wade: alive?*

*justin.k: barely. you kept me up until 4.*

*wade: you kept yourself up. I was just talking.*

We texted through the afternoon. The rhythm was the same as the DMs but faster — shorter messages, less lag, the conversation moving at the speed of two people who had already gotten past the early awkwardness and were now operating in the comfortable zone where you don't have to think about what to say before you say it.

At six he sent a photo.

The Pacific Palisades coastline. Golden light. The ocean flat and bright, reflecting the sunset in a long streak that ran all the way to the horizon. He'd shot it from a balcony — I could see the railing at the bottom of the frame, a half-empty glass of something on the ledge.

One line of text underneath:

*wade: you should see this in person.*

I looked at the photo. I looked at the invitation sitting underneath it, disguised as a casual observation. I looked at it for a long time.

Then I set my phone down and went to practice.
