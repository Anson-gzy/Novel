# Chapter 15: Tiffany, Part Two

*(POV: Zack)*
The first text came three days after surgery.

I was in a hospital bed with my leg elevated in a contraption that looked like it belonged in a science fiction movie — metal rods, foam padding, a pulley system that the nurse adjusted every four hours. The painkillers made the world soft around the edges, and I'd been sleeping in intervals that didn't correspond to any normal human schedule. Three hours on, two hours off, staring at the ceiling or my phone or the window where the same section of sky sat unchanging.

The text was from an unknown number. Three words:

*Is this Zack?*

I knew who it was before I typed the reply. I knew because nobody else from that night would be texting me, and because the three-word structure — flat, minimal, no greeting — sounded like the same voice that had talked to me in the dark for eighteen hours.

*Yeah. Andrew?*

*Yeah.*

*How did you get my number?*

*The hospital had a registry. Families involved. I asked Ethan to find it.*

I looked at the message for a while. Then I typed: *How are you?*

The response took a long time. Not because he was composing something complex — I'd learn later that Andrew's long pauses were almost always about deciding how much truth to release, not about finding the right words. The words were always ready. It was the permission he was negotiating.

*I'm okay.*

Two words. I heard them the way you hear "fine" from someone who is not fine — as a door held open just enough to signal that there's a room behind it, but not enough for you to see what's inside.

I didn't push. I said: *Me too. Leg's held together with metal now. Very cyborg.*

A pause. Then: *That's unfortunate.*

And then, a few seconds later: *The cyborg part is kind of cool though.*

I smiled at my phone. In a hospital bed, with a reconstructed leg and stitches in three places and a headache that wouldn't leave, I smiled. And I knew — in the way you know things that haven't become words yet — that this was going to be something.

---

The texts evolved.

The first week was short messages. Check-ins. *How's the leg.* *Better. Physical therapy starts Monday.* *That sounds terrible.* *It is.* The exchange rate of people who shared something enormous and were now figuring out how to carry it in the regular-sized container of daily life.

By the second week, the messages got longer. Andrew asked about my family — carefully, the way you ask about someone's family when your own has just been catastrophically altered and you don't want the asymmetry to become the whole conversation. I told him about my parents. About my mother's controlled crying in the hospital, which had given way to a fierce, organized efficiency — schedules, appointments, meals delivered to my bedside with military precision. About my father, who'd gone quiet in a way that worried me more than my mother's crying. He'd started checking on me every hour, standing in the doorway of my room without entering, like he needed to verify I was still there but couldn't figure out how to say why.

Andrew listened. Or read, rather. He responded with the kind of attention that made me feel heard in a way I wasn't used to — not performing empathy, just receiving what I said and sitting with it.

I asked about him, too. Not about his parents — not directly. I knew, by then. Not from him. From the news. The gala had been covered extensively in the local press, and the casualty list had been published, and I'd seen the names before the anesthesia had fully worn off. David and Catherine Hayes. I'd read the obituary on my phone in the hospital and felt something shift inside me — not for the people I'd never met, but for the person I'd sat next to in the dark who'd been told they were fine.

I didn't mention it. Andrew didn't bring it up. The subject existed between us like a third presence in every conversation — acknowledged through omission, shaped by the space we built around it. I waited for him to open that door. He didn't. Not in the texts, not in the first few weeks, not until much later, and even then only in the specific, controlled way that Andrew did everything.

What he did talk about was the future. Not in big terms — not college, not careers, not the sweeping kind of future that adults asked teenagers about at dinner parties. He talked about the next few months. About moving in with Ethan permanently. About the decisions being made — by Ethan, by lawyers, by people whose job it was to sort out the logistics of a life that had been restructured without permission. He talked about school.

"I'm not going back to Westbrook," he said one night. Not a text — a phone call. We'd started calling by the third week, usually late, after Ethan had gone to bed and the apartment was quiet and Andrew's voice dropped to the low, even register that I'd first heard in the collapsed building.

"Why not?"

"Because the person who went to Westbrook doesn't exist anymore. I don't want to spend the next two years being the version of myself that everyone there already decided I was."

"Where will you go?"

"Public school. Ethan's already looking into the district."

I processed that. Andrew Hayes — Westbrook Prep, top of his class, the invisible kid who nobody wanted around — voluntarily choosing public school. Not because of money. Because of reinvention.

"That's brave," I said, and meant it.

"It's not brave. It's just the only option that makes sense."

He said it the way he said most things: like the decision had already been made and the emotional component had been filed away somewhere he didn't need to access. But I'd spent enough hours listening to him to know that the flatness in his voice wasn't absence of feeling. It was the sound of feeling being held at arm's length — close enough to acknowledge, far enough not to be overwhelmed.

---

I went to see him in mid-August.

My leg was functional by then — not healed, not pain-free, but operable. I could walk with a brace and a slight limp that my physical therapist said would fade in a few months. My parents drove me to the address Andrew had given me. My mother waited in the car. My father walked me to the lobby and said "take your time" in a voice that meant he was trying to be the kind of parent who lets go, even when letting go required active effort.

Andrew's apartment was not what I expected.

Or rather — I knew, intellectually, that Andrew came from money. Westbrook's tuition was a matter of public record, and the gala itself had been the kind of event where tickets cost more than most people's monthly rent. But knowing it and standing in a fourteenth-floor penthouse with floor-to-ceiling windows and a kitchen that looked like it belonged in a magazine were different experiences.

Andrew met me at the door. He looked different from the last time I'd seen him — which, I realized, had been in the dark, covered in dust, reaching for my hand as they pulled him through a wall. He looked cleaner. More composed. He was wearing a plain white shirt and dark pants, and his hair was slightly longer, and he moved with a self-possession that hadn't been there at Westbrook. Like something had shifted in the architecture of how he carried himself.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

We stood in the doorway for a second that lasted longer than it should have. Then he stepped back and I walked in and the second was over.

---

The day was easy.

That's the thing I keep coming back to when I think about it — how easy it was. After everything we'd been through to get here — the building, the dark, the eighteen hours, the hospital, the weeks of texts and calls — the actual experience of being in the same room with Andrew, in daylight, with clean clothes and working infrastructure and no immediate threat to our survival, was just… easy.

We sat in his room. He showed me a playlist he'd been building — songs he'd found during the weeks of recovery, when the apartment was quiet and Ethan was working and the only thing to do was listen. I put on his headphones and heard the first track and understood immediately why he'd wanted me to hear it — not because the music was significant in itself, but because sharing it was significant. It was Andrew's way of saying: here's something that lives inside my head, and I want you to have it too.

We talked. About the same things we'd talked about in the dark, but different now — lighter, less urgent, stripped of the pressure that comes from not knowing whether you'll be alive the next morning. He told me about Ethan's plans for the apartment. I told him about physical therapy, about the way my therapist made me do exercises that felt pointless until the day they didn't. He laughed at something I said — not the low almost-sound I'd heard at the gala, but an actual laugh, short and surprised, like it had caught him off guard.

I catalogued it. Filed it away in the growing collection of sounds Andrew made when he forgot to be careful.

We ate lunch at the kitchen counter. Ethan wasn't home — working, Andrew said, which apparently happened a lot. The apartment felt large without him. Andrew moved through it with the comfort of someone who had learned its rhythms, opening cabinets and pulling out plates with the automatic ease of a person who did this every day. It struck me that this was his home now. Not the house he grew up in, not the rooms where his parents had been — this sterile, beautiful space where his brother worked and the windows showed a city that kept moving regardless of who had been lost.

After lunch, we sat on the couch and watched something on Ethan's TV — a show neither of us really followed, the kind you put on to have sound in the room. Andrew's phone buzzed once. He looked at it, typed something short, and set it down.

"School starts next week," he said. Not to me, exactly. More to the room.

"Public school?"

"West Valley High. Ethan finalized it yesterday."

"Are you nervous?"

He considered the question with the seriousness he gave everything. "No," he said. "But I'm probably wrong about that."

---

The bracelet was in my pocket.

I'd bought it three days earlier. Not planned — not the result of careful thought or deliberation or any of the processes I usually ran before making a decision. I'd been walking through a mall with my mother — she was looking at something for the house, some post-disaster renovation that she'd channeled her anxiety into — and I'd passed a Tiffany & Co. window and stopped.

There was a bracelet in the display. Silver. Thin. Simple in a way that felt deliberate rather than cheap — the kind of simplicity that requires precision. I looked at it and thought of Andrew. Not for any specific reason. Not because Andrew had ever mentioned jewelry or shown any interest in accessories. I just looked at it and something in my brain said: that's his.

I went in. I bought it. The salesperson put it in a blue box, and I put the blue box in my pocket, and I didn't talk myself out of it, which was unusual for me. I'm the kind of person who talks himself out of things — who weighs the variables, considers the implications, runs the possible outcomes until the impulse that started the process has died of exhaustion. I didn't do that here. The bracelet was in my pocket and the decision was behind me and I was okay with it.

Now I was sitting on Andrew's couch, watching a show I wasn't watching, with the box in my jacket pocket pressing against my hip.

I took it out during a lull. Not during a significant moment — there was no dramatic pause, no meaningful silence. Andrew was looking at his phone again and I just reached into my pocket and pulled out the box and set it on the cushion between us.

Andrew looked at it. Then at me.

"What is this?"

"Open it."

He picked up the box. Turned it over in his hands. The blue was unmistakable — anyone who'd ever seen a Tiffany box would recognize it. He opened it.

The bracelet sat on the white cotton insert, catching the afternoon light from the windows.

Andrew didn't say anything for a few seconds. He looked at the bracelet the way he'd looked at me in the dark — steady, processing, something moving behind his eyes that I couldn't quite read. Then he took it out of the box and put it on his left wrist.

Immediately. Without asking why. Without making a comment about the expense or the gesture or the implications. He just put it on and fastened the clasp and turned his wrist once, watching the chain settle against his skin, and then he looked at me.

"Thank you," he said.

Something happened to me in that moment. Something I didn't have language for then and still don't have clean language for now. It was in the space between what he did — putting on the bracelet instantly, without hesitation, like it was already his — and what it meant. The trust of the gesture. The absence of performance. The quiet declaration that whatever I was offering, he was accepting, fully and without conditions.

I almost said something. I had the sentence ready — not rehearsed, not polished, but present, sitting in my mouth like a breath held too long. The sentence was about what he meant to me. About the dark and the dust and the eighteen hours and the fact that I thought about him every day and didn't want to stop. The sentence was a door, and I had my hand on the handle, and all I had to do was push.

I didn't push.

I let the moment pass. I told myself there was time. Andrew was starting school next week, and he'd be in the city, and we'd keep talking, and the sentence would still be there when I was ready.

"You're welcome," I said.

Andrew touched the bracelet on his wrist — a small gesture, almost unconscious, like he was checking that it was real.

"I like it," he said.

I nodded. And the moment closed, and we went back to watching the show, and the sentence stayed in my mouth like a stone I'd decided not to throw.

---

Andrew started at West Valley High the following Monday.

I didn't ask too many questions about the decision. It was his to make. I understood the logic — Westbrook was the old life, the invisible one, the version of Andrew that nobody had wanted. Public school was the reset. A place where nobody knew him, where the past couldn't follow, where the person he'd decided to become could exist without the weight of the person he'd been.

I respected it. I also felt something I didn't immediately name — a quiet, specific anxiety about what would happen when Andrew walked into a world where I wasn't. At Westbrook, we'd been two people in the same orbit, separated by the social physics of a school that had never connected us. Now we were two people in different orbits entirely, and the connection we'd built in the dark and the hospital and the texts and the phone calls would have to survive the gravitational pull of a hundred new people, new routines, new proximity.

I told myself it would be fine.

The first few weeks, it was. Andrew texted. He called. He told me about the school — the hallways, the classes, the teacher who'd assigned him as some kind of orientation buddy to a student named Justin. He described it the way he described everything: briefly, functionally, with the occasional detail that revealed more than he intended.

"The principal asked a student to look after me," he said during a late-night call, sounding faintly amused. "Like I'm a foreign exchange student."

"Are you being nice to them?"

"I'm being myself."

"That's not the same thing."

A pause. "I know."

The texts continued. The calls continued. Andrew mentioned classes, mentioned his brother, mentioned the adjustment of being somewhere where nobody had preconceptions about him. I listened. I asked questions. I waited for the bracelet to come up in conversation — not because I needed validation, but because I wanted to know if he wore it. I wanted to know if the thing I'd given him had made it from the couch to the rest of his life.

He never mentioned it. Which, with Andrew, could mean anything — that he wore it every day and it had become invisible the way daily objects do, or that he'd put it in a drawer, or that it occupied a private category of meaning that he didn't plan to share.

I didn't ask.

---

The shift started in October. Small. The kind of change you notice the way you notice a temperature change — gradually, then all at once.

Andrew's texts got shorter. Not cold — just briefer. The all-day conversations that had run uninterrupted through September started developing gaps. An hour. Two hours. Then an afternoon. He was busy, which made sense — new school, new classes, the adjustment period that demanded attention and energy. I told myself this was normal. I told myself it would stabilize.

Then he started mentioning someone.

"Justin had a question about my schedule today. It was kind of funny."

Casual. Embedded in a longer message about school. I didn't register it.

A week later: "Justin and I are working on a group project. He's not great at slides but he's trying."

I noticed the name this time. Justin. The student the principal had assigned. The orientation buddy.

A few days after that: "Justin came over after school."

I read that message three times. Then I read it a fourth time. Then I set my phone down and looked at the ceiling of my room and felt something shift — not dramatically, not painfully, but definitively. A recalibration. The specific, quiet adjustment of realizing that a dynamic you'd assumed was stable had started moving without your permission.

Andrew mentioned Justin again. And again. Not constantly — not the way you talk about someone you're obsessed with. But with a frequency that had a rhythm to it, like a word that keeps appearing in a sentence you're writing without you choosing it.

I listened. I paid attention. And I started hearing something in the mentions that I didn't want to hear.
