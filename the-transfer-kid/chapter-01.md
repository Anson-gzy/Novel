# Chapter 1: The Transfer Kid

The group chat had been going off since last night.

I saw the notifications right before I fell asleep — forty-three unread messages in the "West Valley High '24" Instagram group. By the time I actually checked my phone in the morning, it was past a hundred.

I scrolled up to find where the whole thing started.

> **SamiraJ:** guys did u hear?? someone's transferring from Westbrook Prep
>
> **DylanK:** like THE Westbrook? the fancy one in Brentwood?
>
> **SamiraJ:** yep. starts Monday apparently
>
> **TylerMoss:** lol what did he get kicked out
>
> **BeccaLin:** no stfu his parents died. both of them. over the summer
>
> **DylanK:** wait seriously? that's rough
>
> **TylerMoss:** okay yeah that's actually sad
>
> **SamiraJ:** idk the full story but yeah. he's enrolling here now
>
> **MarcusW:** why not just go to another private school lol
>
> **BeccaLin:** maybe he doesn't have money anymore?? use your brain marcus
>
> **TylerMoss:** a prep school kid at west valley. this should be interesting

There were about sixty more messages after that. Half of them were people saying they felt bad for the guy. The other half were already annoyed that some rich private school kid was about to walk into our school and probably act like he was better than everyone.

I read through some of it, liked one of Samira's messages because she seemed to have the most accurate information, and then put my phone down.

I didn't really care either way.

Not in a cold way. I just didn't see how some random transfer affected my life. People transfer in all the time. Most of them blend in within a month and nobody remembers there was ever a conversation about them. I figured this would be the same — two weeks of people talking, then nothing.

I locked my phone, grabbed my bag, and walked to school.

---

So I was wrong about the blending in part.

I first saw him about ten minutes before first period. I was at my locker, digging around for a pen that actually worked, when I noticed the hallway had gone weird. Not loud weird. Quiet weird. The kind of quiet that happens when a bunch of people stop talking at the same time and start watching something.

I turned around, and there he was.

He walked down the hallway like he'd been going to this school for years. Not nervous. Not looking around. Not holding a folded schedule printout and checking room numbers like every other new kid I'd ever seen. He just walked — at his own pace, shoulders back, bag slung over one arm. He had dark hair, kind of messy in a way that looked like he'd meant it. Plain black jacket over a white shirt. Nothing special. But there was something about the way he carried himself. Like the hallway belonged to him and the rest of us just happened to be in it.

A crowd was forming. Not a big dramatic circle — more like people had stopped walking and turned their heads, one by one, until there were fifteen or twenty of them just standing there. Some of them whispered. Most of them just watched.

"Is that the transfer?" someone behind me asked.

I didn't answer, because it obviously was. Who else walks through a school hallway with twenty people watching them and doesn't even blink?

A few of the football guys were leaning against the wall near the gym entrance. I heard one of them mutter "private school" under his breath, and the one next to him started doing this fake British accent — "Pardon me, where is the polo field?" — which was incredibly stupid, but the guys around him laughed.

The transfer kid didn't react. Not even a glance in their direction. He walked past them the way you walk past a wall. Not avoiding. Not engaging. Just moving forward like they were part of the building.

That was the part that got me. I'd seen new kids ignore people before, but it was always obvious they were trying — jaw tight, eyes fixed ahead, pretending they didn't hear. This was different. This was a guy who genuinely seemed like none of it reached him. Like the staring, the whispering, the twenty people who had stopped their morning routines to look at him — none of it registered.

I leaned against my locker and watched him turn the corner toward the admin office, and I realized I'd been standing there with my locker open for way too long.

Arrogant. That was the word that came to mind. I didn't know if it was fair, but that was my first impression.

---

I got called to the principal's office during fourth period.

This is never a good sign. I'm not a troublemaker or anything, but I'm also not the kind of student principals call in for congratulations. My grades are fine — C-plus average, low B's when I actually try, which isn't as often as it probably should be. So when Mrs. Delgado's voice came through the classroom speaker asking for Justin Herrera, my first thought was: what did I do now?

My second thought was: did Tyler put my name on something again?

I walked in and Principal Nakamura was sitting behind his desk with that specific expression adults get when they're about to ask you for a favor you can't say no to. Patient. Already decided. Just waiting for you to catch up.

"Justin," he said. "Have a seat."

I sat down.

"You've probably heard we have a new student. Andrew Hayes."

"Yeah," I said. "I think everyone's heard."

He nodded. Not the kind of nod that means anything — just the kind that fills the space between sentences. "Andrew's situation is unique. He's transferred from Westbrook Preparatory, and he's been through a significant personal loss recently. I won't go into the details."

I already knew the details. Everyone did. Instagram moves faster than any official briefing.

"I'd like someone to help him adjust," Nakamura continued. "Show him around. Check in from time to time. Make sure he's not lost — physically or otherwise."

I waited for the part where he explained why that someone was me.

"I think you'd be a good fit for that," he said.

I almost laughed. "Me?"

"You're social. You get along with a lot of different people. And you're approachable."

"Approachable?"

"You are." He said it like it was a fact he'd already confirmed from multiple sources. "I'm not asking for a huge commitment, Justin. Just keep an eye on him. Make an effort. If he needs something, point him in the right direction."

I sat there trying to think of a reason to say no that didn't sound selfish. I didn't have anything against Andrew. I didn't know Andrew. That was sort of the problem — I had no reason to care about this, and Nakamura was sitting there looking at me like I was the kind of person who cared about things like this.

The silence went on a little too long, which I think was the point. Nakamura is good at that. He doesn't push. He just waits, and eventually you do it to yourself.

"Yeah," I heard myself say. "Okay. Sure."

He smiled. "I appreciate it."

I walked out of there wondering what exactly I'd agreed to and why I felt like I'd just signed something I hadn't read.

---

"Wait," Dylan said at lunch, grinning already. "You're his babysitter?"

"I'm not his babysitter."

"Nakamura literally assigned you to take care of him. You're a babysitter."

"I'm just supposed to check in on him. Show him around. That's it."

Samira looked up from her phone. "He doesn't really seem like the type who needs showing around, though. He walked through the building this morning like he had the floor plan memorized."

"That's what I'm saying."

"Also," she added, "isn't he in all AP classes? Like, every single one?"

"AP English, AP History, AP Bio, AP Calc," Tyler listed, counting on his fingers. "I heard he walked into AP Chem and already knew the lab equipment by name."

"Great." I stabbed my fork into whatever the cafeteria was calling pasta that day. "So they want me — a solid C-plus student who used SparkNotes for last week's reading — to guide the all-AP kid. Because that makes perfect sense."

"Maybe they think he'll rub off on you," Dylan said.

"Maybe they think you'll bore him into transferring back," Tyler offered.

"You're both incredibly helpful."

Samira put her phone down, which meant she was about to say something she actually meant. "People in the group chat are already saying he's stuck up. Like, multiple people tried talking to him today and he barely responded."

That tracked with what I'd seen in the hallway. The way he'd walked past everyone without a flicker of acknowledgment. But I didn't say anything, because I wasn't totally sure that walking through a hallway without making eye contact counted as stuck up. It could've been something else. Something I didn't have a word for yet.

"Anyway," I said, pushing the pasta around. "It's whatever. I'll say hi to him a couple of times and that'll be the end of it."

Dylan raised his water bottle. "To Justin's promising career in social work."

I kicked him under the table.

But walking home that afternoon, I kept thinking about the hallway. Not about Andrew specifically — just about the way the crowd had formed around him and how he'd passed through it like it was air. I'd never seen someone manage that on their first day. Not without trying. Not without even seeming to notice.

I still thought arrogant was the right word. But it sat funny in my head, like a jacket that was almost the right size but not quite.
