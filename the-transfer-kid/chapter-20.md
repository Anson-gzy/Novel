# Chapter 20: Scroll

*(POV: Zack)*

I opened my eyes.

The ceiling was the same ceiling. White, flat, a hairline crack running from the light fixture to the corner that I'd been meaning to mention to my mom for three months. My phone was next to me on the bed, screen dark. Andrew's last text was still on it somewhere. *I texted Justin.* I didn't need to look at it again.

Six thirty on a Saturday. Light still coming through the blinds. My body felt heavy — not tired, just weighted, like the air in the room had gotten denser while I wasn't paying attention.

I got up. Went to the bathroom. Splashed water on my face. Looked at myself in the mirror for about two seconds, which was one second more than I usually bothered with, and went back to my room.

I wasn't going to be the guy who lay in bed all night thinking about it. That wasn't how I worked. When I tore my rotator cuff sophomore year, I sat in the trainer's office, listened to the diagnosis, asked how long, and started planning around the gap. No crying. No "why me." You get the information, you adjust, you move. That's the whole process.

This was the same thing. Different muscle. Same process.

---

I tried doing homework for about twenty minutes. AP Lit. Some essay on symbolism in *Their Eyes Were Watching God* that I'd been putting off since Tuesday. I read the same paragraph four times without retaining a single word and then closed the laptop.

I tried watching something on my phone. A highlight reel from a recent swim meet — some kid from Palo Alto who'd broken the 200 IM state record. I watched him touch the wall and throw his fist up, water flying off his arm, and I felt nothing. Usually that kind of thing got my pulse going. Tonight it was just a guy in a pool.

I scrolled past it.

Instagram's algorithm is a weird thing. You feed it enough data — enough likes, enough pauses, enough three-second hovers on a post you didn't actually tap — and it starts building a version of you that's more honest than you are. It knows what you look at when nobody's watching. It knows what you slow down for.

I had two accounts. My real one — *zackchen_* — had maybe four hundred followers, mostly swim team, some kids from school, a few cousins my mom had made me follow back. I barely posted. A race photo here, a group shot there. The kind of account that tells you nothing about a person except that they exist and occasionally go outside.

The other account had no name, no posts, no followers. I'd set it up months ago for scrolling without leaving tracks. The handle was some random string the app had generated. I used it when I wanted to look at things without anyone knowing I was looking.

I switched to the blank account.

The feed was different here. No friends, no swim stuff, no algorithm shaped by social obligation. Just pure consumption data — whatever I'd paused on, whatever I'd watched twice, whatever I'd let play past the three-second mark without swiping away.

I scrolled. Cars. Food. A guy doing pull-ups with bad form. A corgi running into a glass door.

Then a face.

The video autoplayed before I could decide whether to watch it. A guy — my age, maybe a year older — sitting cross-legged on the floor of a big kitchen. Nice kitchen. Marble countertops, stainless steel everything, the kind of pendant lights my mom would have opinions about. He was half-Asian — I clocked it immediately the way I always did, the quick unconscious scan for features that matched mine. Sharp jawline. Hair that was doing its own thing. Wearing a hoodie that cost either twelve dollars or three hundred, impossible to tell.

He was trying to build a shelf. Not a real shelf — one of those flat-pack IKEA things that comes with a bag of hardware and a wordless instruction manual. He had pieces spread around him on the tile floor like a crime scene.

"Here's my theory," he said, looking straight at the camera with zero expression. "The Swedes design these things to be slightly impossible on purpose. It's a national sport. They sit in Stockholm watching Americans cry on YouTube and they laugh. That's the whole economy."

He grabbed a wooden dowel and lined it up with a hole that was clearly the wrong size. Pushed. It didn't go. He pushed harder. The dowel snapped clean in half.

He held up both pieces and stared at them.

"Shit."

One word. No mugging, no exaggerated reaction. Just the flat, genuine frustration of a person who'd made an honest attempt and gotten an honest result. I felt my mouth pull sideways. Almost a smile. The first one in a couple hours.

I tapped his profile. *waderichardson*. 1.2 million followers. Verified. Bio: *I break things.*

I scrolled through his grid. It was all like that — direct, physical, completely free of the polished influencer thing where every frame looks like an ad for a life nobody actually lives. He rated gas station snacks with dead seriousness. He complained about the specific frustration of having long arms and a short torso while clothes shopping. He tried to cook things and sometimes they came out wrong and he ate them anyway.

In a few of the videos, there was someone else. A tall guy, blonde, always slightly off to the side or in the background. Not performing for the camera, not avoiding it either. Just present. In one clip Wade was talking about the worst coffee he'd ever had, and the blonde guy walked through the frame behind him carrying a laundry basket, and Wade pointed at him without turning around and said, "Moon agrees. Moon has opinions about everything but won't say them on camera because Moon thinks he's above this."

"I am above this," the guy said, off-screen. His voice was flat and dry.

"See? Opinions."

The caption on that video tagged *@moonthompson*. I tapped it. Private account. No bio. But the picture in the circle was clearly the same guy — blonde, tall, good-looking in a way that was more architectural than warm.

They were together. The body language was obvious if you knew what to look for, and I always knew what to look for. The way Wade's posture shifted when Moon was in the room. The way Wade's jokes got slightly sharper when he had an audience of one. Not performing *for* Moon. Performing *near* Moon. There's a difference.

I watched maybe forty minutes of his stuff. My thumb kept moving. The weight in my chest was still there, but it had shifted — moved to the side, made room for something else. Not happiness. Just engagement. My brain was tracking Wade's voice the way it tracked a pace clock during intervals: steady, rhythmic, giving me something to sync to.

I hit the bottom of his recent posts. A photo from two weeks ago: Wade on a balcony, ocean behind him, a half-empty glass of wine in his hand. Late light. The kind of golden hour that Pacific Palisades gets in October when the marine layer burns off early. He was looking at the camera with an expression I couldn't read — not posing, not candid. Something in between. Like he'd been thinking about something specific and the camera had caught the tail end of it.

Caption: *Tell me something I don't know.*

Eight thousand comments. I didn't read them.

I tapped the DM icon.

The cursor blinked in the empty message field. White space. I stared at it.

This was stupid. The guy had over a million followers. He probably got three hundred DMs a day from strangers who wanted to tell him he was funny or hot or both. He wasn't going to read a message from a blank account with zero followers and a generated username.

I looked at my profile. *user4782901*. No photo. No posts. No bio. The digital equivalent of an empty room.

Something moved in my chest. Not the heavy thing — something else. Smaller. Darker. An impulse that formed before I could evaluate it, the way your hand moves to catch a ball before your brain decides whether to try.

Andrew was talking to Justin right now. Maybe texting. Maybe calling, the way he'd called me an hour ago with that crack in his voice. Andrew was out there, reaching toward someone new, and the someone had a name, and the name was Justin, and I was sitting here in my room with a cracked ceiling and a dead homework assignment and forty minutes of a stranger's content still playing in my head.

I tapped Edit Profile. Deleted *user4782901*.

Typed *justin.k*.

I didn't think about it. Thinking about it would have meant asking why, and why would have meant admitting something I didn't want to look at directly. So I just did it. The way you put on someone else's hoodie when you're cold — not because it's yours, not because it means anything, just because it's there and it fits and you need something between you and the temperature.

I hit save. Went back to Wade's DM thread. Typed the first thing that came to mind.

*The dowel goes in the other piece first. The manual isn't cowardly, you're just impatient.*

I sent it.

Set the phone face-down on the mattress. Got up. Went to the kitchen. Opened the fridge, looked at the shelves without seeing anything, closed it. Drank a glass of water. Stood at the counter for a minute. The house was quiet. My parents were at dinner — the Harringtons, Pacific Palisades, the monthly thing they did with people who owned the same kind of cars and had the same kind of opinions about wine.

My phone buzzed from the bedroom.

I heard it through the wall. One short vibration against the sheets. Probably nothing. Probably a notification from some app I forgot to mute.

I walked back to my room. Picked up the phone. Turned it over.

*Instagram: waderichardson sent you a message.*

My heart rate ticked up. Not anxiety — the chemical thing that happens at the blocks, right before the starter says *take your marks*. Adrenaline finding its way into a system that had been running flat all evening.

I opened it.

*waderichardson: interesting. an empty account with zero posts and a strong opinion about swedish furniture. who the hell is justin?*

I started to type a response. Stopped. Started again. Deleted it.

Then a gray bubble popped up at the bottom of the screen.

Three dots. Bouncing.

He was already typing something else.
