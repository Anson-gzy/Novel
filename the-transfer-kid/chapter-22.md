# Chapter 22: Delivered

*(POV: Zack)*

Monday.

*wade: rate this. one to ten. be honest.*

A video. Behind-the-scenes footage of him trying to film a bit about grocery store sushi. Three failed takes in a row — the first one, the sushi fell apart before he could pick it up. The second one, his phone fell off whatever he'd propped it on. The third one, he picked up the sushi, took a bite, and made a face so genuinely disgusted that he broke character and said "oh, that's actually terrible" while still chewing.

*justin.k: the face on take three. that's the content. skip the first two.*

*wade: you think?*

*justin.k: nobody wants to watch you be funny on purpose. they want to watch you fail. the face was real. use that.*

He didn't respond for a minute. Then:

*wade: huh. you're right. that's annoyingly good advice from a stranger.*

*justin.k: we've been talking for two days. I'm not a stranger.*

*wade: fine. annoyingly good advice from an acquaintance with no profile picture and a suspicious level of media literacy.*

I set the phone down. Went to school.

---

Tuesday.

I'd built the Justin character without meaning to. It wasn't a plan — more like improv. Every time Wade asked something specific, I filled in a detail, and the details accumulated into a person who was almost real.

Justin went to a public school in the Valley. His family was normal — two parents, no drama, the kind of household where you ate dinner together most nights and nobody mentioned money because there was enough of it and not too much. He drove a used Honda Civic. His best friend was loud and talked too much. He liked swimming but his school's team wasn't competitive enough to matter.

Some of it was true. I did swim. I did have a loud friend — though in my case it was Aaron Chen, no relation, who I'd known since club team at thirteen. But the rest — the public school, the Civic, the "normal" — was fiction. Borrowed loosely from the version of Justin that Andrew had described during those late-night calls.

The irony was right there if I wanted to look at it. I was pretending to be the person Andrew had chosen over me. Playing a character built from the scraps of the life Andrew found more interesting than mine.

I didn't look at it. I texted Wade back.

*justin.k: what are you doing right now?*

*wade: editing. which means sitting in front of my laptop cutting the same three seconds of footage over and over while questioning my life choices. you?*

*justin.k: studying for an AP Lit exam. which means staring at a book while questioning mine.*

*wade: we're basically the same person in different tax brackets.*

I huffed a laugh at that. He had no idea.

---

Wednesday.

Wade's texts came in waves. Sometimes three in a row, rapid-fire, his thoughts spilling out in fragments. Sometimes nothing for two hours, then a single long message that read like he'd been composing it in his head the whole time.

He sent me an outtake reel. Six minutes of footage that would never make it to his channel — him burning eggs, tripping over a cord, arguing with his editing software, sitting in his car in a parking lot eating a breakfast burrito in complete silence for forty-five seconds because "sometimes you just need to sit in a parking lot and eat." The outtakes were better than the finished product. Rawer. More him.

*justin.k: you should post the outtakes. seriously. the polished stuff is good but this is the version of you that people would actually connect with.*

*wade: you sound like a manager.*

*justin.k: I sound like someone who just watched you eat a burrito in a CVS parking lot and found it genuinely compelling.*

*wade: that might be the nicest thing anyone's ever said to me.*

At nine that night:

*wade: Moon's going to New York on Thursday. Some shoot. Editorial thing. He'll be gone through the weekend.*

I read the text. Read it again. The information sat there — neutral on the surface, loaded underneath. Moon would be gone. The house would be empty. The weekend was open.

I didn't respond for ten minutes. Not because I was deciding what to say. Because my body was doing something my brain hadn't authorized — pulse ticking up, shoulders dropping, a looseness in my chest that felt like anticipation. The physical response arrived before the thought. My body had already decided. My brain was just catching up.

*justin.k: sounds quiet.*

*wade: too quiet. I don't do well with quiet. come over friday. stay the night. I'll cook.*

There it was. Typed out. No ambiguity. Not "we should hang out sometime" or "maybe you could come by." Come over Friday. Stay the night. I'll cook. Three sentences, each one more specific than the last, each one closing the distance between a thing we'd been circling and the thing itself.

*justin.k: what are you cooking?*

*wade: pad thai. it's the only thing I'm good at. say yes.*

*justin.k: yes.*

*wade: good. here's the address.*

A pin dropped into the chat. I tapped it. The map expanded.

Pacific Palisades. Castellammare Drive. The blue dot sat on a hillside street that curved along the bluffs above the ocean, lined with houses that had gates and hedges and the kind of views that real estate agents called "unobstructed."

I knew this road.

Not well — not the way you know your own street. But the way you know the streets adjacent to places your parents take you. The Harringtons lived on Castellammare. Three houses up, maybe four, in a Mediterranean-style thing with terracotta tiles and a pool that Mrs. Harrington complained cost more to heat than her first apartment.

My parents had dinner there monthly. I'd been inside that house maybe a dozen times. Eaten Mrs. Harrington's overcooked salmon. Sat on their patio with their son, Eric, who was my age and spectacularly boring and whose idea of rebellion was drinking a second glass of sparkling water when his parents weren't looking.

Wade lived three houses away from the Harringtons.

I sat on my bed and looked at the blue pin on the map. Three miles from my parents' social circle. Three miles from people who knew my real name, my real school, my real parents' car in the driveway. Three miles from the world where I was Zack Chen, Westbrook Prep swimmer, the kid whose parents survived the gala, the boy who'd been Andrew Hayes's first real thing.

On Friday night, I was going to drive to that street and walk into Wade Richardson's house and be Justin. And the gap between who I was and who I was pretending to be was going to shrink from a screen to a door.

I zoomed out on the map. Stared at the blue pin for another minute.

Then I closed the app and went to brush my teeth.
