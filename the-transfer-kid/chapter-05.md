# Chapter 5: Probably a Party

I made it to lunch before the interrogation started.

Not even to my seat. I was still walking across the cafeteria with my tray when Dylan called out from our usual table, loud enough for people three tables over to hear:

"Yo, Justin. Your boy said hi to someone in AP Chem today. Like, voluntarily. People are losing their minds."

I sat down. "He's not my boy."

"He said good morning to the teacher," Samira said, scrolling through her phone. "And then apparently nodded at someone in the hallway. Actual eye contact. People are posting about it."

"People are posting about a guy nodding?"

"This is Andrew Hayes we're talking about," Tyler said. "The dude went two weeks without making a facial expression. One nod and people think he joined a cult."

Dylan leaned forward. "But here's the thing. Here's the actual thing." He looked at me with the expression he gets when he's about to say something he knows will annoy me. "Multiple sources say he's been friendlier this week. Specifically this week. And what else happened this week? Oh right — you started spending time with him in History."

"We sit next to each other in one class."

"You're his assigned companion. His court-appointed friend. And suddenly the guy starts acting human? That's you, man. That's the Justin Herrera effect."

"That's not a thing."

"It's absolutely a thing."

Samira looked up from her phone. There was something in her expression — not teasing, not the way Dylan was doing it. More like she was watching me think. "He invited you over, right?"

I froze. "How do you know that?"

"Becca heard it from the girl who sits behind you in History. The one with the green notebook."

I made a mental note to never say anything within a ten-foot radius of the girl with the green notebook ever again.

"It's not a big deal," I said. "We're working on the project."

Tyler raised an eyebrow. "At his house?"

"People work on projects at each other's houses."

"Yeah, but people don't usually invite the class C-plus student to do the work part." Tyler popped a chip into his mouth. "He already did all the research. What's he need you there for?"

"Maybe he just wants to hang out?" Dylan said it like it was a punchline, but underneath the grin there was an actual question. He looked at me. "I mean, it's kind of weird, right? He doesn't talk to anyone. He doesn't eat lunch with anyone. And then he invites you over?"

I didn't answer right away. Because Dylan was right — it was kind of weird. I'd been trying not to think about it in those terms, but hearing it said out loud by someone else made it harder to avoid.

"He clearly likes you," Dylan said.

"He doesn't like me."

"The evidence suggests otherwise."

"We've had two conversations."

"Three, technically. The hallway, the class, and then today when he asked you over. That's three more than he's had with anyone else at this school."

I opened my water bottle and drank from it so I'd have a reason not to talk for a few seconds.

Here's what I didn't say. Here's what I was thinking while Dylan laid out his evidence and Tyler threw chips at the table and Samira watched me with her quiet, careful eyes:

For the past few days, I'd been carrying something I hadn't shown anyone. A private, stupid, completely irrational feeling that the attention from Andrew was specific. That it was aimed. That when he said "hey" to me in class this morning, it wasn't just a hey — it was a hey that he chose, that he pointed in my direction, that he would not have pointed at anyone else.

I hadn't said this out loud because it sounded insane. But I'd been holding it, quietly, like a small warm thing in my pocket.

And now, sitting here listening to my friends, that warm thing was starting to cool.

Because what if it wasn't specific? What if Andrew was just doing this — waking up, opening up, deciding to be a person — and I happened to be nearby? The girl in AP Chem got a good morning. Someone in the hallway got a nod. Maybe I was just another someone. Maybe the invite was nothing. Maybe he was doing this with five different people and I was the only one dumb enough to think it meant something.

"Justin?" Samira said.

"What?"

"You went somewhere."

"I'm right here."

She didn't push it. She just looked at me for a beat, then went back to her phone. Dylan started arguing with Tyler about whether the cafeteria pizza counted as real food, and the conversation moved on.

But I didn't move with it. I sat there, smile in place, eating my lunch like everything was fine, while something inside me shrank and shrank and shrank.

---

After school, I walked home.

The rational part of my brain was being very loud and very reasonable. *It's just hanging out. It's just a project. You are overthinking every single aspect of this and you need to stop.*

The other part of my brain — the part that didn't have a name, the part that had been running background calculations about Andrew Hayes for a week — was saying something different. Something I couldn't hear clearly, but could feel in my chest.

I got home. Dropped my bag in the hallway. Went to my room.

I looked at my closet.

I wasn't going to change. That would be ridiculous. You don't change your clothes to go work on a history project at someone's house. You wear what you wore to school. It means nothing. You're just going over. It's casual.

I changed my shirt.

Then I looked in the mirror and changed it back. Then I changed it again — different shirt this time, a dark blue one that fit better. I rolled the sleeves once. Twice. Unrolled them. Rolled them once.

This was pathetic. I was being pathetic. I was standing in my room rearranging my sleeves for a guy who'd invited me over to probably look at slides about the Cold War.

I sat down on my bed.

My phone was next to me. Screen dark. No notifications. Andrew hadn't texted me since the invite — hadn't texted me at all, actually, because we'd never exchanged numbers. He'd just said "my place" and "after school" and that was it. No address. No time. No "I'll text you the details."

Wait.

I didn't have his number. He didn't have mine. He hadn't given me his address.

How was I supposed to get there?

I picked up my phone and stared at it. I could DM him on Instagram — I followed him now, and I'd noticed yesterday that he'd followed me back. A quiet, unremarkable follow. No like on any of my posts. Just the follow, sitting there in my notifications like a period at the end of a sentence.

But I didn't know what to say. "Hey, what's your address?" felt too direct. "Are we still on for today?" felt too much like I cared, which I did, but he didn't need to know that. "What time?" felt okay but also felt like only half a message, and sending half a message was worse than sending nothing.

I typed: *Hey, I realized I don't have your address. Want to send it over?*

I deleted it.

I typed: *What's the plan for today?*

I deleted it.

I typed: *Hey*

I stared at the three letters. Then I deleted them too.

The clock on my phone said 3:42. School had been out for twenty-seven minutes. Andrew walked east on Maple every day at 3:15, which meant he'd been home for at least fifteen minutes by now. He was somewhere in an apartment with an older brother who'd dropped out of college, probably already settled in, probably not thinking about this at all.

And I was sitting on my bed in a blue shirt I'd changed into twice, holding an empty text box, not moving.

What if he forgot? It was possible. He'd said it at the bell, almost like an afterthought. "Do you want to come over after school?" Like it wasn't a plan, just a thought that had crossed his mind and left his mouth before he'd committed to it. What if he was home right now, doing homework, and the invitation had already slipped out of his memory the way small things do when they don't matter to you?

What if I showed up and it was a group thing? What if he'd said the same thing to three other people and this was going to be a whole gathering, and I'd walk in with my rolled sleeves and my carefully chosen shirt and realize I was just one of five?

What if I was just one of five?

The doubt sat in my stomach like something cold and heavy. I could feel it pulling me down, anchoring me to the mattress. The reasonable thing was to message him. The simple, normal, not-weird thing was to open Instagram, type "what's your address," and press send.

But there was something underneath the logistics — something that had nothing to do with addresses or slide decks or the Cold War. Something I didn't know how to say, even to myself, sitting alone in my room with nobody watching.

I wanted to go. I wanted it in a way that felt stupid and big and too sharp to look at directly. Not because of the project. Not because Nakamura asked me to check in. Not because I had any reason that could survive someone asking me "why do you care?"

I just wanted to go. And I was terrified of what that meant.

My phone screen went dark. I didn't unlock it.

I sat there — dressed, ready, not moving — and let the silence fill up around me like water.
