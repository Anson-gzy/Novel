# Chapter 17: Three Dots

*(POV: Justin)*
Something was different.

I noticed it the way you notice a draft in a room — not immediately, not with alarm, just a slow awareness that the temperature had changed and you couldn't locate the open window.

Andrew still responded to my texts. Still said hey in the hallway, still showed up to World History on Tuesdays and Thursdays with his laptop and his notebook and his clean handwriting. Still made the occasional dry comment during class that almost sounded like a joke if you knew what to listen for. He was there. He was present. He was doing all the things that Andrew did.

But there was a gap where the warmth used to be.

I couldn't have pointed to it if someone had asked. It wasn't in the words — the words were the same. It wasn't in the timing — he still texted back within an hour, still picked up when I called. It was somewhere underneath the surface, in the quality of his attention. Like he'd taken the dial that controlled how much of himself he gave you and turned it down by ten degrees, so gradually that you didn't feel the change until you were already cold.

I told myself it was nothing. People have off weeks. People have internal weather that has nothing to do with you, and the narcissistic thing — the thing I was actively trying not to do — was to assume that every shift in someone else's behavior was a response to something I had or hadn't done.

I told myself this for about a week. Then I stopped being able to tell myself things.

---

It started with the phone.

Andrew had always been someone who kept his phone face-down during conversations — on his desk in class, on the table at lunch if he was sitting with people, on the arm of the couch when we were hanging out. It was one of those things I'd noticed without analyzing. Andrew keeps his phone face-down. Andrew doesn't check it mid-sentence. Andrew gives you his full attention when he's with you, and the phone stays in the background like an appliance that's been turned off.

That changed.

It wasn't dramatic. He didn't suddenly start scrolling through Instagram in the middle of our conversations. But I noticed — because I noticed everything about Andrew, because my brain had been running a continuous observation protocol on this person since the day he walked into World History and said "sure" — that his phone was face-up more often now. That he glanced at it in moments that used to be uninterrupted. That sometimes, mid-conversation, his eyes would shift down for a half-second and then come back to me, and the half-second felt like a door opening and closing on a room I wasn't allowed to see.

Once, during class, I saw the typing indicator on his screen. Three dots, appearing and disappearing and appearing again. Someone was texting him. Someone who typed and deleted and typed again, the way you do when you're trying to get a message right.

Andrew looked at the dots. Then he typed something back — quick, two thumbs, the efficient keystroke pattern of someone who knew exactly what they wanted to say. Then the phone went face-down again, and he turned back to his notebook, and the moment was over.

I didn't ask who.

I wanted to ask who. The question sat in my throat like something swallowed wrong — not painful, just present, lodged in a place where it demanded attention every time I tried to think about something else. Who are you texting? Who are you giving the face-up phone to? Who gets the quick response when I get the half-second glance?

But asking would mean admitting that I was tracking his phone behavior, which would mean admitting that his phone behavior mattered to me, which would mean admitting something about the geometry of my feelings that I was not ready to put into a sentence.

So I didn't ask. I just watched. I just noticed. I just filed the information in the mental folder that was getting so thick it was starting to bend.

---

The Marcus situation reached me through the group.

Not directly — nobody sat me down and said "here's what's happening." It came in fragments, the way information moves through a friend group: a comment here, a look there, the slow assembly of a picture that nobody was painting on purpose.

It started with Dylan. We were walking to the parking lot after school — or rather, I was walking and Dylan was keeping pace while delivering one of his extended analyses of something that had happened in his second-period class. I was half-listening, which was my default state with Dylan — present enough to respond to the punchlines, not so present that I was absorbing the details.

Then he said: "Marcus has been weird lately."

I looked at him. "Weird how?"

"I don't know. Quiet. More than usual. And you know Marcus — his baseline is already pretty quiet. When he gets quieter than that, it means something is going on in that giant analytical brain of his."

"Maybe he's just stressed about finals."

"Maybe." Dylan didn't sound convinced. "But he's doing the thing where he observes people without blinking. Like he's studying them. And it's — I think it's about Andrew."

The name landed in my chest with a specific, familiar weight. "What about Andrew?"

"I don't know. That's the thing. He won't say. But he's been asking about him — casually, you know, the Marcus version of casual, which is basically a police interrogation with better sentence structure. He asked Becca if she'd heard anything about Andrew on Instagram. He asked me if I'd seen Andrew around."

"He has Government with Andrew."

"I know. Which makes it weirder. He's seeing Andrew four days a week and still asking other people about him."

I didn't say anything. Dylan looked at me sideways.

"You okay?"

"Yeah."

"You sure? Because you're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you say 'yeah' like you're reading it off a teleprompter."

"I'm fine, Dylan."

He let it go. We split at the corner, and I walked the rest of the way home with the information sitting in my head like a puzzle piece I didn't want to fit into the picture.

---

Samira confirmed it without confirming it.

This was Samira's specialty — the non-confirmation confirmation. The look that said more than a paragraph. The silence that contained an entire essay.

We were at lunch. Tuesday. The table was at its usual density — Dylan talking, Becca on her phone, Tyler eating with the steady cadence of someone who treated meals as a mechanical process. Marcus was across from me. Quiet. His specific kind of quiet — the analytical kind, the kind where you could almost hear the hard drive spinning.

I'd been watching Marcus for a few days by then. Not consciously — or not at first. But once Dylan had flagged it, I couldn't stop seeing it. Marcus was watching Andrew. In class, in the hallway, in the peripheral moments between periods. He was running the same observation protocol on Andrew that I'd been running for weeks, and the parallel made me uncomfortable in a way I couldn't immediately name.

Samira was sitting next to me. At some point she said, quietly enough that only I could hear: "You're staring at Marcus."

"I'm not staring."

"You've been looking at him for two minutes."

I shifted my eyes to my tray. "He's been acting weird."

"Yeah."

"Do you know why?"

She didn't answer immediately. She took a bite of her salad — the precise, unhurried kind of bite that Samira used as punctuation, a way of creating space between a question and a response she was deciding whether to give.

"I have a theory," she said.

"What theory?"

"It's not my thing to share."

"Since when do you have things you don't share?"

She looked at me. Not the pointed look — the other one. The one that was gentle underneath its sharpness, the one that said: I see what's happening to you, and I'm choosing not to make it worse by giving you information you're not ready for.

"Since now," she said. And went back to her salad.

I sat there with my tray and my questions and the growing weight of things that people knew and I didn't, and I started running the math in a direction I didn't like.

---

Here's the math.

Andrew was pulling away from me. Gradually, without announcement, the way a tide goes out — not all at once, but in slow increments that you don't notice until you're standing on dry sand.

Marcus was paying attention to Andrew in a way that had changed from casual to specific. More than just sitting in the same Government class. More than just noticing. Something had shifted in how Marcus looked at Andrew — not hostile, not suspicious, but focused. Intent. The focus of someone who wanted something and was trying to figure out how to get it.

Samira knew something. Samira always knew something, but this time the something had a weight to it, a do-not-touch quality that she normally reserved for situations that could hurt people.

And Andrew — Andrew was checking his phone. Andrew was typing and deleting and typing again. Andrew was giving someone the face-up phone and the quick response and the shifted attention that used to be mine.

The math said: Marcus and Andrew. Something between them. Something that started in Government and leaked into the hallways and the phone screen and the gap in Andrew's warmth.

I didn't like the math. The math made my chest tight and my sleep bad and my thoughts circular. But the math was consistent, and I couldn't make it add up to anything else, and the thing about being honest — even with yourself, even when the honesty hurts — is that you can't unsee a number once you've run the equation.

---

I went quiet about it.

Not the quiet where you withdraw from everything — I still showed up to lunch, still hung out with the group, still performed the daily requirements of being a functioning person with friends and classes and a life that needed maintenance. I was there. Just not all the way.

I didn't confront Marcus. What would I say? "Are you interested in Andrew?" And then what — he'd say no and I'd feel stupid, or he'd say yes and I'd feel something worse than stupid?

I didn't confront Andrew either. Not because I didn't want answers — I wanted answers so badly that the wanting had become its own kind of pain, a low, constant ache behind my sternum that flared whenever Andrew looked at his phone or Marcus went quiet at the table. But confronting Andrew would require naming what I was feeling, and naming what I was feeling would require admitting that the thing between us — the penthouse, the playlist, the "I trust you," the whole careful, fragile architecture of whatever we'd been building — mattered to me in a way that went beyond friendship.

I wasn't ready for that. Not because I didn't know what I felt — I'd known for weeks, maybe longer, in the pre-verbal way you know things before your brain translates them into sentences. I knew it when I changed my shirt twice before going to his apartment. I knew it when I lay in bed after the penthouse visit and couldn't sleep. I knew it when the sound of his voice on the phone made something in my chest rearrange itself.

I knew it. I just couldn't say it. And as long as I couldn't say it, I couldn't confront it, and as long as I couldn't confront it, I was stuck in the loop — watching, waiting, getting increasingly bad at sleeping.

Marcus was at the table every day. Also quiet, but a different kind of quiet. The kind I'd started noticing in the last week — the quiet of someone who had received information he was digesting. Not angry. Not sad. Just processing. Running his own internal calculations in that precise, analytical brain of his.

I looked at him and looked at Andrew and looked at my phone and looked at nothing, and the days kept moving, and the temperature kept dropping, and I kept waiting for something to happen that would make the math make sense.

Nothing happened. The not-happening was worse than anything that could have happened, because at least with an event you could react to it. With nothing, you just sat with the shape of your own anxiety and watched it grow.

December came. I stopped sleeping well. I started waking up at 3 AM and lying in the dark and running through conversations in my head — things I could say to Andrew, things I could ask Marcus, things I could tell Samira that would make her give up whatever she was holding behind that gentle, sharp look. I never said any of them. I just rehearsed them in the dark, and the rehearsal became its own kind of company, and the company wasn't good but it was better than the silence.

I was fine. I kept telling myself I was fine. The word had become a reflex — fine, fine, fine — a sound I made without thinking, the social equivalent of breathing. Dylan asked if I was okay and I said fine. Samira looked at me and I said fine. Marcus sat across from me at lunch and didn't ask anything, which was either mercy or indifference, and I said fine to the question he didn't ask.

Fine. Fine. Fine.

None of them believed me. I didn't believe me either. But the word kept coming, automatic and empty, and I kept saying it, and the days kept moving, and something that had been building for weeks — in the phone screen and the typing dots and the gap in Andrew's warmth — kept building, and I waited for it without knowing what it was.
