# Chapter 3: Mutual Followers

Dylan had a theory that every embarrassing thing you'd ever done was stored somewhere on the internet, just waiting for the worst possible moment to resurface.

He proved this on Wednesday afternoon by pulling up a video from Tyler's birthday party last summer — the one where I'd tried to do a backflip off the diving board and landed sideways in what can only be described as a full-body belly flop. Someone had filmed it. Someone had posted it. And someone — Dylan — had saved it to his camera roll like a weapon.

"Watch his face," Dylan said, holding his phone out to Samira. "Watch right before he hits the water. That's the face of a man who knows he's made a mistake."

"I look like I'm being abducted," I said.

"You look like a starfish having a seizure," Tyler added, which was rich considering it was his pool and he'd been the one yelling "do it, do it, do it" from the shallow end.

Samira watched the video twice, laughing harder the second time. "Justin, your arms. Why are your arms like that?"

"I panicked."

"You panicked before you left the board. You were panicking while you were still standing on it."

"Can we talk about literally anything else?"

We were sitting on the bleachers near the track, doing the thing we always did after school on days when none of us had anywhere to be — which was most days. Just sitting, phones out, talking about nothing. This was my favorite version of my friend group. No drama, no pressure, just four people being stupid in the sun.

But I was quieter than usual. I didn't know why, exactly. Or I did, but I didn't want to look at it directly. There was something sitting in the back of my head — a low background noise, like a song you can't name but can't stop humming. I laughed when I was supposed to. I threw insults at Tyler when he deserved them. But part of me was somewhere else, and I didn't know where.

Eventually they left. Dylan had dinner. Tyler had practice for something he was definitely going to quit within the month. Samira had a study group she described as "organized suffering."

I walked home alone.

---

The thing about Instagram is that it makes everything feel casual. You follow someone, they follow you back, nobody thinks anything of it. It's just what you do. It doesn't mean anything.

That's what I told myself when I opened the app, typed "Andrew Hayes" into the search bar, and hit follow.

His profile was sparse. Fifty-two followers. No bio. A plain profile picture — not even a selfie, just a close-up of something I couldn't identify. A texture. Maybe concrete. Maybe a wall.

He had eight posts. I scrolled through all of them in about thirty seconds.

The first few were from over a year ago — what looked like the view from a window, some kind of cityscape at dusk. A coffee cup on a table. A page of a book, photographed at an angle so you couldn't read the words. The kinds of photos that felt intentional but didn't tell you anything. Like someone curating an image of themselves that was designed to reveal exactly nothing.

Then a gap. Nothing posted for eight months.

Then, two weeks ago, a single photo: an empty hallway. Not our school — somewhere else. Older building, darker hallway, fluorescent light at the end. No caption.

I stared at that one longer than I should have. Something about it made my chest feel heavy, and I didn't know why. It was just a hallway. I scrolled back up, closed the app, and put my phone face-down on my bed.

That was it. That was all I was going to do.

Except it wasn't.

---

Over the next two days, I started noticing things.

Not on purpose. That's what I kept telling myself. I wasn't watching Andrew. I was just — aware of him. In the way you become aware of someone once they've taken up a certain amount of space in your head, whether you asked them to or not.

I noticed he always came from the east hallway in the morning. Always before the first bell but never more than five minutes before it. He didn't rush. He didn't linger. He arrived like someone who had calculated the exact amount of time needed and saw no reason to waste any of it.

I noticed he sat in the same seat in every class I'd heard about — back corner, near the window if there was one. I noticed he ate lunch alone, but not in the cafeteria. I never actually saw him eat. He just wasn't in the cafeteria when I looked, which I was apparently doing now — looking.

I noticed he left school at exactly 3:15, every day, walking east on Maple toward the residential streets. I knew this because my locker was near the front entrance and I happened to be at my locker at 3:15.

I happened to be at my locker at 3:15 three days in a row.

That's when I realized I was doing something I hadn't agreed to do. I was paying attention. Not the kind Nakamura asked for — the checking-in, showing-around kind. This was different. This was the kind of attention you give to a problem you can't solve, where you keep staring at it hoping the answer will appear if you just look long enough.

I didn't like that I was doing it. But I couldn't seem to stop.

---

Thursday night, lying on my bed doing absolutely nothing productive, a thought showed up from nowhere.

Where does he go?

Not after school — I knew that direction already, even though I was pretending I didn't. I meant at the end of the day. Where does he go home to? His parents were gone. Both of them. Over the summer. That was the thing everyone talked about in the group chat the first day but nobody had mentioned since, like it was old news now that they'd moved on to opinions about his personality.

But it wasn't old news. It was the thing underneath everything. The private school. The transfer. The blank face and the one-word answers and the way he walked through crowds like they were empty rooms. All of it sat on top of something I'd somehow managed to not think about until right now.

Both parents. Gone.

I picked up my phone and opened the group chat, scrolling back to the first day. Becca had been the one who said it: *his parents died. both of them. over the summer.* Just like that, in lowercase, between Tyler asking if he got kicked out and Dylan saying "that's rough."

I put the phone down and stared at the ceiling.

There had to be someone. You can't be sixteen and live alone. Somebody would've called the school. Forms would've been signed. There was always an adult somewhere in the paperwork, even if you never saw them.

The next day, between classes, I asked Samira. Not directly — I wasn't going to walk up and say "hey, do you know Andrew's living situation?" That would've sounded insane. I did it the way you do everything in high school: sideways.

"That transfer kid," I said, like I was barely remembering he existed. "Do we know anything about his family? Like, is he staying with relatives or something?"

Samira looked at me with the expression she gets when she knows you're fishing for something. "Why?"

"Just curious."

"You're never 'just curious.'"

"I literally am."

She stared at me for two more seconds, then shrugged. "Becca said he's living with his older brother. I think the guy's like twenty-two, twenty-three? Dropped out of college or something to take custody."

I nodded. "Huh."

"That's all you're going to say? 'Huh'?"

"What am I supposed to say?"

She gave me another look — the Samira look, the one that says *I see exactly what you're doing even if you don't* — and went to class.

I stood there in the hallway for a minute after she left.

An older brother. Twenty-two, twenty-three. Dropped out of college. Took custody of a sixteen-year-old who'd just lost both parents and was transferring from a private school he probably couldn't afford anymore.

I tried to imagine what that looked like. The apartment — because it would be an apartment, not a house. Some place in the neighborhood near school. A twenty-two-year-old trying to be an adult for someone who'd decided to stop needing anyone.

I didn't want to think about it. But I thought about it the entire walk home.

---

"So," Dylan said the next day at lunch, with the tone that meant he was about to be annoying. "How's the babysitting going?"

"I'm not babysitting anyone."

"Have you shown him where the gym is yet? The nurse's office? The vending machine that takes Apple Pay?"

"I've talked to him twice."

"Yeah, but like..." Dylan leaned back, grinning. "It's kind of funny, right? Nakamura picks you, out of everyone. Like, of all the people at this school, he looked around and said, 'Yeah. Justin Herrera. That's the guy I trust to handle the socially complex transfer student from a prep school.'"

Tyler laughed. "Maybe it's because you're the least threatening person in our grade."

"That's not a compliment."

"It's not supposed to be."

"He probably just needed someone who wouldn't scare the guy off," Samira said, not looking up from her phone. "You're basically harmless."

"Thanks. Really. All of you. Incredible support system."

The conversation moved on. Tyler started talking about his new theory that the school was going to cut the art program, and Dylan called him a conspiracy theorist, and Samira kept scrolling through something on her phone with half a smile.

I let it go. I changed the subject. I ate my sandwich and laughed at the right moments and did exactly what I always do.

But something had shifted in the way I carried it. The teasing didn't land different — it was the same jokes they'd been making since the first week. What was different was me. I didn't want to talk about Andrew with them. Not because it was private. Not because there was anything to protect.

Just because the things I knew — the hallway he came from, the time he arrived, the older brother, the empty Instagram feed — felt like they belonged somewhere else. Somewhere quieter. Somewhere the jokes couldn't touch them.

I finished eating, balled up my bag, and tossed it at the trash can. Missed.

Dylan held up a "6" with his fingers, like a judge at the Olympics.

I flipped him off.

But walking to my next class, I thought about it again. The eight-month gap in Andrew's posts. The single photo of an empty hallway. The brother who dropped out of college.

I knew more about Andrew Hayes than I had any reason to know. And the uncomfortable part wasn't that I'd gone looking for it. The uncomfortable part was that I wanted more.
