# Chapter 6: Penthouse

My phone rang.

Not buzzed. Not lit up with a notification. It rang — the actual ringtone I'd never bothered to change from the default, the one that sounds like a phone from a commercial. Nobody calls me. My mom texts. Dylan sends voice notes when he's too lazy to type. The only person who's called me in the last month was a scam about my car's extended warranty, and I don't have a car.

I looked at the screen. Unknown number. LA area code. I stared at it for two full rings, trying to figure out who would be calling me at four in the afternoon on a Thursday, and then picked up because not picking up felt like a decision I wasn't ready to explain to myself.

"Hey. It's Andrew."

I sat up straight so fast my phone almost slipped out of my hand.

"Hey," I said. My voice came out normal, which was a miracle, because inside my chest something had just detonated.

"I wasn't sure you'd pick up," he said. "People don't really answer calls anymore."

"Yeah." I was walking around my room now, no destination, just moving. "I — yeah. I almost didn't."

"Fair enough."

A pause. I could hear something in the background on his end — not music, not TV. Just the general ambience of a space. A window open, maybe. Traffic from somewhere high up.

"So," he said. "Do you still want to come over?"

The question hit different over the phone. In class, said at the bell, it had felt like a thought that escaped before he could catch it. Here, on a phone call he'd initiated, it was deliberate. He'd thought about it. He'd picked up his phone, found my number, and called.

Wait.

"How do you have my number?" I said.

A beat of silence. Not the uncomfortable kind — the kind where someone decides how much information to give you.

"I asked around," Andrew said.

"You asked around."

"I got it."

That was it. That was the answer. He'd asked around, and he got it, and the rest was apparently not his problem. It was such an Andrew response that I almost smiled, which was concerning because I was alone in my room and nobody was watching.

I should have pushed it. In any other situation — with any other person — I would have said "asked who?" and gotten a real answer. But something about the way he'd already moved past it made me move past it too. Like he'd drawn a small line in the sand and I'd decided, without really deciding, to respect it.

"So," he said again. "I can come get you. If you want."

"You can — what?"

"Pick you up. I'm driving."

I looked down at myself. The blue shirt. The rolled sleeves. The outfit I'd changed into twice. At least that part was already handled.

"Yeah," I said. "Okay. Yeah."

"Cool. Send me the address."

He hung up. Not "see you soon." Not "give me twenty minutes." Just — cool, send me the address, click. I stood there holding my phone for a few seconds, then texted my address to the unknown number and saved the contact.

I typed "Andrew" and paused before I could add a last name. Then I typed "Hayes" and stared at it. Andrew Hayes. Sitting in my phone now, like a permanent thing. Like a person who might call again.

I put the phone in my pocket, checked my hair in the bathroom mirror, and went downstairs to wait.

---

He pulled up twelve minutes later.

I was sitting on the front steps, which I'd been doing for about eleven of those twelve minutes, because apparently I am the kind of person who goes outside to wait for someone almost immediately after they say they're coming. I'd brought my earbuds but hadn't put them in. Couldn't focus on music. Couldn't focus on anything.

The car that turned onto my street was not what I expected.

I don't know what I expected, exactly. Something old, maybe. Something practical — a used sedan, a hand-me-down from the brother. Something that matched the picture I'd been carrying in my head: the modest apartment, the twenty-two-year-old holding things together, the kid who transferred to public school because private school wasn't possible anymore.

What pulled up to my curb was a black BMW. Not flashy — no tinted windows, no custom rims, nothing that screamed money from across the street. But clean. New-looking. The kind of car where you could tell, just from the way the door closed, that it cost more than everything currently sitting in my room combined.

I didn't say anything about it. I got in, put my bag between my feet, and said "hey" like this was normal. Like I got picked up in BMWs on Thursday afternoons all the time.

"Hey," Andrew said. He was wearing the same thing he'd worn to school — black jacket, white shirt — but he looked different behind the wheel. More settled. More like a person who existed in a context, instead of a person who appeared in hallways and disappeared after the bell.

He pulled away from the curb. The ride was quiet for about thirty seconds.

"Do you drive?" he asked.

"No. I mean — I have my permit. I just never go anywhere that's not walkable."

He nodded. "LA's not really a walking city."

"It is if you don't have a car."

That got the smallest thing from him. Not a laugh. Not even a smile. Just a sound — a short exhale through his nose that I had never heard him make before. An almost-sound. A shadow of amusement.

I looked out the window and let myself absorb that for a second.

The drive was short — ten minutes, maybe less. Andrew drove the way he did everything: calm, no wasted movement, slightly too confident for a sixteen-year-old. He asked me about classes. Not in a small-talk way — he actually listened when I answered, and followed up, and didn't make me feel like my standard-level schedule was something to be embarrassed about.

"Reeves assigned a new reading," I said. "Did you see the email?"

"I saw it. Sixty pages by Tuesday."

"Sixty? I thought it was forty."

"Sixty. Check again."

"That's disgusting."

"It's not great," he agreed, and there it was again — that tone. Not warm, not cold. Just normal. Just a person talking to another person like it was the most natural thing in the world.

I started to relax. Not all at once — not a decision I made. My shoulders dropped a half-inch. My hands unclenched on my knees. I stopped rehearsing what I was going to say next and just said things when they came to me. Andrew didn't fill every silence, but the silences he left didn't feel empty. They felt like part of the conversation. Like he was comfortable with them, and because he was, I was.

I didn't notice we'd arrived until he slowed down.

---

The building was tall.

Not tall like a regular apartment complex — tall like it had opinions about the skyline. Glass and steel, a lobby visible through the front entrance with the kind of lighting that made everything look like a hotel. There was a doorman. An actual doorman, standing behind a desk in the lobby, who nodded at Andrew like he saw him every day.

Because he probably did.

Andrew parked in an underground garage — the kind with assigned spots and painted numbers on the concrete. His spot said 14B. We took an elevator. Andrew pressed the button for the fourteenth floor and the doors closed without anyone else getting on.

I was very carefully not reacting to anything.

The hallway on fourteen was quiet. Thick carpet. Wall lights instead of the usual fluorescent strips. Andrew stopped at a door, used a key — not a code, a physical key — and pushed it open.

I walked in and my brain went blank.

This was not an apartment. I mean, technically it was an apartment — four walls, a ceiling, furniture. But it was the kind of apartment that existed in a different category from any apartment I had ever been inside. The ceiling was high. The windows were floor-to-ceiling, covering the entire far wall, and beyond them was Los Angeles — not the version I lived in, with strip malls and bus stops, but the version from the photos. The skyline version. The one you see from the hills at night and think, yeah, I guess people actually live up there.

The living room was open-plan, connected to a kitchen with a marble island and appliances that looked like they'd never been used. There was a couch — a real couch, long and dark gray and the kind of deep you sink into. A rug that looked soft enough to sleep on. Shelving built into the walls with books arranged by height. Art on the walls — actual art, not posters. Two small paintings with the kind of frames that cost money just to look at.

I stood in the doorway and took it all in.

"You can come in," Andrew said, already walking toward the kitchen.

I came in. I put my bag down next to the door, because it felt weird to carry my beaten-up backpack any further into this place. Like it would lower the property value.

"Do you want water? Or — I think there's juice."

"Water's fine."

He opened a cabinet — not the fridge, a cabinet — and pulled out a glass. A real glass, not plastic. He filled it from a filter on the counter and handed it to me.

"Thanks," I said, and then stood there holding it like I'd never held a glass before.

Everything I had imagined about Andrew's living situation — everything I'd built in my head over the past week — was wrong. The modest apartment. The sparse furniture. The twenty-two-year-old brother barely holding things together in a two-bedroom somewhere near school. The narrative I'd written, where Andrew's family money was gone and the transfer was about starting over with nothing — all of it collapsed in the time it took me to walk from the front door to the kitchen.

This was a penthouse. Andrew Hayes lived in a penthouse.

---

"Ethan," Andrew said, gesturing toward the living room. "My brother."

I looked. A guy was sitting at the dining table, which was being used as a desk — laptop open, two phones next to it, a legal pad with handwriting I couldn't read. He was maybe twenty-three, twenty-four. Dark hair like Andrew's, a little shorter. He wore a plain gray T-shirt and looked like someone who'd been working for several hours and had forgotten what time it was.

He looked up when Andrew said his name.

"Hey," he said to me. Pleasant. Not overly friendly — just the kind of greeting you give to someone who's been mentioned to you before. "You're Justin?"

"Yeah. Hi."

"Ethan." He extended a hand across the table without standing up, and I shook it. His grip was firm and brief. "Andrew's told me about you."

I glanced at Andrew, who was opening the fridge like the conversation had nothing to do with him.

"Good things, I hope," I said, because that's what you say when someone tells you that, even though the phrase has never once produced an honest answer.

Ethan smiled — a real smile, small but genuine. "He said you asked if the Berlin Blockade was the same thing as the Berlin Wall."

I felt my face get warm. "That is not something I wanted repeated."

"It's a reasonable question," Ethan said, in a tone that made it clear he didn't think it was a reasonable question at all but was being polite about it.

Then he went back to his laptop. Not abruptly — just smoothly, like the social interaction was complete and he had things to do. I watched him for a second. His eyes moved across the screen with the same quick focus Andrew had when he worked. Whatever was on that laptop wasn't homework. Spreadsheets. Multiple tabs. Something that looked like a contract or a proposal in one of the windows.

This was not a college dropout holding things together by a thread. This was someone running something. A business, a venture, a whatever — I didn't know the word. But I recognized the posture. The two phones. The legal pad. The complete lack of anyone telling him what to do.

Andrew walked past with two glasses and nodded toward the hallway. "My room's this way."

I followed, carrying my water, trying to keep my internal monologue at a reasonable volume. It was not cooperating.

Andrew's brother was not what I pictured. The apartment was not what I pictured. The car was not what I pictured. Nothing — literally nothing — about what I was seeing matched the story I had written in my head over the past week. The one where Andrew transferred because the money ran out. The one where the public school was a downgrade, a necessity, a last resort.

The money had not run out. The money was clearly fine. The money was marble countertops and a BMW and a penthouse on the fourteenth floor with a view that made the whole city look small.

So why did he transfer?

If it wasn't about money — and it obviously wasn't — then what pushed him out of Westbrook Prep? What made a kid with this kind of life walk into a public school and start over from scratch?

I didn't ask. I filed it away in the same mental folder where I kept everything else about Andrew that I wasn't ready to say out loud. The folder was getting thick.

---

Andrew's room was the most normal part of the apartment.

Not messy — Andrew didn't seem capable of mess. But lived-in. A bed that was made but not perfectly. A desk with a laptop and a stack of books. Shelves with more books, a few photo frames turned face-down, a small speaker. The walls were mostly bare except for one print — something abstract, black and white, nothing I recognized.

He dropped onto his desk chair and opened his laptop. "You can sit wherever."

I chose the edge of the bed, because the desk chair was taken and the floor felt like it would make me look weird. The mattress was comfortable in a way that made my own bed feel like a slab of concrete by comparison.

"What do you want to watch?" he asked, turning toward me with his laptop balanced on one knee.

"I don't care. Whatever you want."

He opened something — a show I'd heard of but never watched. We didn't talk about why he picked it. We didn't negotiate. He just put it on and angled the screen so we could both see, and we watched.

It was easy. That was the thing. Sitting in Andrew's room, on Andrew's bed, watching something on Andrew's laptop while the city glowed through the window — it was easy in a way that nothing about Andrew had ever been easy before. He wasn't performing. He wasn't doing the cool, untouchable thing he did at school if he was around more people. He was just sitting there, legs stretched out, occasionally making a comment about the show that was so dry I almost missed that it was a joke.

At one point I laughed at something on screen and he looked at me. Not the analyzing look — just looked. Like he was noticing what I looked like when I laughed.

I stopped laughing and pretended I was still watching the show.

An hour passed. Maybe more. The show played. We talked about nothing — the kind of nothing that felt like more than nothing, if that makes sense. He told me the speaker on his shelf was the first thing he bought with his own money. I told him my room was half the size of his and I'd had the same bedsheets since freshman year. He didn't laugh at that, but he breathed out through his nose again — that almost-sound — and I catalogued it like a scientist recording a rare observation.

Then he did something small.

He got up, walked to his desk, and pulled open a drawer. He took out a pair of headphones — not earbuds, actual over-ear headphones, matte black, the kind I'd looked at online once and then closed the tab when I saw the price.

"Here," he said, and tossed them onto the bed next to me. "Try these."

I put them on. He pulled up a playlist on his phone — no name, no cover, just a list of songs I didn't recognize. He pressed play.

The first song filled my head. Something soft. Instrumental at first, then a voice — low, scratchy, singing in a language I didn't know. The headphones were so good that I could hear the breath between the words, the slight rattle of the recording equipment, a chair creaking somewhere in the background of the studio.

I took them off after about thirty seconds. "These are insane."

"They're decent," he said, which was Andrew for "I know."

"What is this song?"

"Something I found a while back. I have a playlist."

He didn't offer to share it. He didn't not offer, either. He just sat there while I put the headphones back on and listened to the second track, which was completely different — heavier, something with drums — and I realized that this was the first time Andrew had shown me anything about himself that wasn't accidental. The bracelet had slipped through. The "I trust you" had slipped through. But this — the headphones, the playlist, the invitation to listen — this was deliberate. This was a corner of his life, offered on purpose, with both hands.

I listened to three more songs. When I finally took the headphones off, Andrew was sitting at his desk with his legs up, reading something on his laptop. He didn't look over. He didn't ask if I liked them. He just let the moment exist without turning it into something that needed a response.

I set the headphones on the bed and said, "I should probably head out."

"The Metro station is three blocks east. Take the Red Line."

"You're not going to drive me?"

"I could. But you'd miss the sunset from the platform. It's good this time of year."

I looked at him, trying to figure out if he was joking. His face gave me nothing. Standard Andrew.

"Okay," I said. "The train it is."

I grabbed my bag from the front hall. Ethan was still at the table, still working, but he looked up long enough to say "nice meeting you." Andrew walked me to the door. He didn't say goodbye — he said "see you Tuesday," which was the day we had History together, which meant he'd already thought about when we'd see each other next.

I walked to the Metro station. The sun was going down over the city, orange and pink bleeding into the smog line, and the platform was mostly empty. I stood there with my bag on one shoulder and waited for the train.

And I felt — I don't know. I don't have a word for it. Not sad, exactly. Not happy. Not nervous the way I'd been nervous getting ready. Something lower than all of that. Something that had settled into my chest while I wasn't paying attention and now sat there like a stone.

I had spent an evening in Andrew's apartment. I'd listened to his music. I'd met his brother. I'd sat on his bed and watched a show and talked about nothing and it had felt like the most natural thing in the world, and now I was standing alone on a train platform and the distance between here and there felt like something I could measure in my body.

The train came. I got on. I sat by the window and watched the city slide past, block by block, expensive streets giving way to regular ones, the buildings getting shorter and the lights getting harsher and the world returning to the version I actually lived in.

I got home. Put my bag down. Went to my room. Lay on my bed — my regular bed, with the freshman-year sheets and the pillow that was too flat — and stared at the ceiling.

I was in trouble. I didn't know what kind yet, but I could feel it — the shape of it, pressing in from every side, the way you can feel weather changing before the sky does.

Andrew Hayes had let me into his world for two hours, and now my world felt smaller.

I rolled over and looked at my phone. No new messages. No missed calls. Just the contact I'd saved earlier — Andrew Hayes — sitting in my phone like proof that tonight had happened.

I closed my eyes and didn't sleep for a long time.
