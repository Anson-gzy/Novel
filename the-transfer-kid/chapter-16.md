# Chapter 16: Go Find Him

*(POV: Zack)*
The thing about listening — really listening, the kind where you don't just process the words but track the architecture underneath them — is that you start hearing things the other person doesn't know they're saying.

Andrew mentioned Justin the way you mention weather. Naturally. Without preface. The name slipped into conversations the way rain slips into a forecast — present, unremarked upon, as though its being there required no explanation.

"Justin said something funny in class today."

"Justin came over to work on the project."

"Justin doesn't know how my phone number ended up in his contacts. I had someone at school get it for me."

That last one stopped me. We were on the phone — a Tuesday night, late, the usual time. Andrew was in his room, and I was in mine, and between us was the entire topography of a city neither of us could see. He said it casually. Like it was a logistical detail. Like acquiring someone's phone number through a third party was a normal step in a normal friendship.

"You asked someone to get his number?" I said.

"Marcus. A guy in my Government class. He seemed like the kind of person who'd keep it quiet."

"Why didn't you just ask Justin yourself?"

A pause. The kind of pause I'd learned to read — not empty, but full of a calculation I couldn't see.

"I don't know," Andrew said. "I think I wanted it to seem — less deliberate."

I lay in my bed and stared at the ceiling and processed that sentence. Less deliberate. Andrew, who did everything deliberately, who moved through social space with the precision of someone who had designed every interaction before it happened — Andrew wanted something to seem less deliberate. Which meant the thing itself was deliberate. Which meant the thing itself mattered to him enough to warrant camouflage.

I didn't say any of this. I said: "Did you call him?"

"Yeah. He almost didn't pick up."

"But he did."

"He did."

The conversation moved on. We talked about something else — Ethan's work, my physical therapy, a song I'd found that I wanted Andrew to hear. Normal things. Easy things. The surface of our conversation was undisturbed.

Underneath it, I was running a calculation of my own.

---

The mentions accumulated.

Not in a straight line — Andrew wasn't the kind of person who talked about someone constantly, who built a verbal monument to their presence in his life. It was more subtle than that. Justin appeared in Andrew's sentences the way a color appears in a painting — not as the subject, but as part of the palette. Background at first. Then midground. Then, gradually, impossible to ignore.

"Justin noticed the bracelet today."

I held my breath when he said that. We were texting, which was both better and worse — better because he couldn't hear my silence, worse because the words sat on my screen and didn't move.

*The bracelet I gave you?* I typed.

*Yeah. He asked about it. I told him someone gave it to me.*

*What did he say?*

*He asked who. I said someone who isn't around anymore.*

I read that three times. "Someone who isn't around anymore." That was how Andrew described me to Justin. Not by name. Not as a partner or a friend or any category that would anchor my existence in Justin's understanding. Just — someone. Not around. Anymore.

I set my phone down. Picked it up again. Put it down. Picked it up.

*That's accurate*, I typed.

*It's the simplest version*, Andrew replied.

I wanted to ask: is there a more complicated version? Do I exist in a category that requires more than one sentence? When you look at the bracelet on your wrist, do you think of the building or the hospital or the couch or all of it?

I didn't ask any of that. I said: *How's the project going?*

And the conversation moved on, and the bracelet stayed on Andrew's wrist, and Justin stayed in Andrew's sentences, and I stayed where I was — in a bedroom across the city, listening to the sound of someone I cared about caring about someone else.

---

I tried the indirect route first.

This was my natural mode — I didn't do confrontation. I did careful, lateral approaches. Questions that left room for the other person to say what they needed to say without being cornered into it.

"You've been hanging out with Justin a lot," I said one night. Phone call. Late. The usual configuration.

"Yeah." Neutral. Andrew at his most Andrew — functional, unrevealing.

"Is that good?"

"It's fine. He's — he's different from what I expected."

"Different how?"

A long pause. I could hear Andrew thinking — or rather, I could hear the shape of the silence that thinking created.

"He's honest," Andrew said finally. "In a way that people at Westbrook weren't. He says what he's feeling even when he doesn't mean to. He can't help it. It's like — his face does things before his brain can stop it."

I processed that. The specificity of the description. The attention to mechanism — not just what Justin was, but how he operated. This was not the way you described someone you were casually hanging out with. This was the way you described someone you'd been studying.

"He sounds interesting," I said.

"He is."

The conversation went awkward. A beat too long. A question I didn't ask sitting in the air between us. Then Andrew said something about Ethan and the moment passed and we recovered, and by the end of the call we were back on safe ground — music recommendations, weekend plans, the comfortable surface of two people who knew each other well enough to navigate away from the difficult parts.

This happened more than once.

I'd bring up Justin. Andrew would respond with something that revealed slightly more than he intended. The conversation would stiffen, then relax, then move on. Each time, I recalibrated. Each time, the picture got a little clearer.

Andrew was interested in Justin. Not interested the way I'd been interested in Andrew — not the slow, deliberate recognition that had grown from a collapsed building into something with a pulse. Andrew's interest was different. It was chaotic. Unplanned. The kind of interest that happened to you rather than something you chose, and Andrew — who chose everything, who designed every interaction, who built his entire reinvention on the principle of control — didn't know what to do with something he couldn't control.

He didn't know what to do with Justin.

And because he didn't know what to do with Justin, he was doing nothing. He was sitting with the feeling the way he sat with everything — patiently, analytically, at arm's length. Waiting for it to resolve. Waiting for his brain to catch up with whatever his chest was doing.

I recognized this. I recognized it because I'd done the same thing. In a hospital bed, with a reconstructed leg, looking at three-word texts on a cracked screen. I'd sat with the feeling and waited for it to make sense, and it never did, and eventually I'd accepted that some things don't make sense — they just are.

---

The decision built slowly.

I didn't wake up one morning and decide to let Andrew go. It wasn't a single moment of noble sacrifice, not a dramatic renunciation. It was more like a series of small adjustments — tiny shifts in the way I held the situation, each one releasing a fraction of the pressure I'd been carrying.

Andrew and I were together. That was the fact. We'd been together since the hospital — not officially, not with a label, but in the way that two people who had survived something together and then spent months building a private world between them were together. We called every night. We texted every day. He wore my bracelet. I had his voice in my ear as the last sound before sleep.

But Andrew was also somewhere else. Partly. In a way that he wasn't naming and I couldn't force him to name. He was at West Valley High, and at West Valley High there was Justin, and Justin had gotten into the spaces that I occupied through a different door — not through survival and darkness and eighteen hours of enforced honesty, but through proximity and warmth and the slow, daily accumulation of someone being present in your life.

I could have held on. I could have waited. I could have kept the bracelet and the phone calls and the late-night conversations and let the Justin thing play out on its own, because maybe it would resolve — maybe Andrew would realize that what he had with me was deeper, more tested, more real than whatever he was feeling for a boy he'd known for a few weeks.

But I knew Andrew. I knew him the way you know someone after sitting in the dark with them for a day and a night with nothing between you and the truth. And I knew that Andrew — the real Andrew, the one underneath the reinvention — was not someone who could want something and not pursue it. He would sit with it, yes. He would hold it at arm's length, yes. But eventually the wanting would overwhelm the holding, and when it did, Andrew would either act on it or bury it so deep that it would damage something inside him that I didn't want damaged.

I didn't want to be the reason Andrew buried something.

---

It happened on a Saturday.

Not late at night — mid-afternoon. Andrew had called, which was unusual for a Saturday. Usually we texted during the day and called at night. But he'd called, and his voice had a quality I hadn't heard before — not his flat analytical register, not his late-night honest register. Something closer to uncertain. Like the architecture of how he usually operated had a crack in it and he was standing at the edge of the crack, looking down.

We talked about nothing for twenty minutes. Then I said it.

"Andrew."

"Yeah."

"If there's someone — at school, or wherever — if there's someone you want to spend time with, you should."

Silence. Not a pause — silence. The kind where you can hear the other person's breathing change.

"I'm saying this as a friend," I continued. The word felt wrong in my mouth — too small, too standard, a container that couldn't hold what I was putting in it. "If there's something you're figuring out, you don't need permission from me. You have it. Whatever it is."

"Zack —"

"I mean it."

More silence. I could hear him processing — the specific texture of Andrew's silence when he was running through implications, testing the weight of what had just been said, deciding how to respond.

"There's someone," he said. Quiet. The quietest I'd ever heard him.

"I know."

"I didn't — I haven't —"

"I know."

"It doesn't change —"

"I know, Andrew."

He stopped. I heard him breathe. One breath. Two. The sound of someone letting go of something they'd been gripping too tightly.

"His name is Justin," he said.

"I know."

A pause. Then, in a voice that was almost a question: "Are you okay?"

I thought about it. Really, genuinely thought about it, the way Andrew had taught me to think about things — not performing an answer, not giving the response the situation called for, but actually checking the internal landscape and reporting what I found.

"I don't know," I said. "But I will be."

"Zack."

"Yeah."

"Thank you."

I nodded. He couldn't see me nod, but I did it anyway, because some responses belong to the body and not the voice.

"Go find him," I said.

The call ended. I set my phone down on the bed and sat there for a while. Not doing anything. Not feeling anything sharp or specific. Just sitting with the shape of what I'd just done, the way you sit with a piece of furniture you've rearranged — looking at the new configuration, registering that the room is different now, deciding whether it's better or worse and realizing that the question itself isn't quite right.

I didn't regret it. Not exactly. Regret requires believing that a different choice would have led to a better outcome, and I wasn't sure a different choice existed. Andrew was going to feel what he felt for Justin regardless of what I did. My options were to stand in the way of it or to step aside. Standing in the way would have worked for a while — weeks, maybe months — but eventually the pressure would have built and something would have broken, and the something that broke might have been us.

So I stepped aside. And the stepping felt like falling, but the falling felt necessary, and necessary is not the same as good but it's the only word I had.

My phone buzzed. A text from Andrew.

It said: *I texted Justin.*

I read it. I set the phone down. I stared at the ceiling.

Then I closed my eyes and let the silence have the room.
