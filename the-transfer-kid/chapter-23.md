# Chapter 23: Pacific Palisades

*(POV: Zack)*

I drove my mom's Audi.

That was the first problem. Justin — the real Justin, the character I'd built — drove a used Honda Civic. I'd said so. Wade might not remember, but I did, and the Audi Q5 sitting in the driveway was the opposite of a used Civic. It was pearl white, leather interior, a navigation system that spoke in a calm female voice that my mom had named "Patricia" for reasons she'd never explained.

I thought about taking an Uber. That would have been smarter — no car to explain, no license plate, no evidence. But Ubers left receipts, and receipts left trails, and I was already deep enough into this without adding a paper trail that connected Zack Chen's credit card to Wade Richardson's address on a Friday night.

So I took the Audi. Parked it a block south, around the curve where the road dipped and the hedges were thick enough to hide a car from the street. Walked the last two hundred feet.

The house was exactly what I expected and nothing like what I expected. Modern — flat roof, floor-to-ceiling glass, clean lines, the kind of architecture that looks like it was designed by someone who thinks walls are optional. It sat on the bluff side of Castellammare, facing west. Through the front windows I could see straight through to the ocean. The sunset was happening behind the house, throwing everything inside into warm gold light.

I stood at the front door for about five seconds. Adjusted my jacket. Checked my phone — no new messages. Exhaled.

Rang the bell.

The door opened. Wade was barefoot. Gray hoodie, the same one from the IKEA video or one identical to it. Black shorts. His hair was damp, like he'd showered recently and hadn't done anything about it after. He looked at me the way you look at something you've been expecting — no surprise, just confirmation.

"Justin," he said.

"Hey."

"You're tall."

"So are you."

He smiled. Not the camera smile — something smaller, less produced, the kind that uses only one side of the mouth. He stepped back and held the door open.

"Come in. Don't slip — the floors are stupid."

The floors were polished concrete. Cool under my feet after I took off my shoes — instinct, not instruction. He noticed.

"You take your shoes off," he said. It wasn't a question.

"Yeah."

"Most people don't. Moon doesn't. He walks around in his shoes like a barbarian and I've given up saying anything about it."

"It's a floor thing," I said. "My mom would kill me."

"Chinese mom thing."

"Yeah."

"Yeah."

We stood in his kitchen. It was the same kitchen from the videos — marble counters, pendant lights, the stainless steel appliances — but bigger in person. Everything was bigger in person. The ceilings were high enough to lose heat. The living room opened into a wall of glass that framed the ocean like it was hung there on purpose.

Wade was already cooking. Two pans on the stove, a cutting board with vegetables half-chopped, a bottle of fish sauce open on the counter. The air smelled like peanut oil and lime.

"Pad thai," he said. "As promised. If it's bad, lie to me."

"I won't lie."

"Then if it's bad, don't say anything. I'll read your face."

I leaned against the counter and watched him cook. He moved the way he moved in his videos — efficient, physical, no wasted motion. His knife work was decent. Not trained, but practiced. He'd done this enough times that his hands knew the shapes without his brain needing to supervise.

"Wine?" he said, reaching for a bottle on the counter without looking at me. Red. Something French — I caught the label. Saint-Émilion. Not cheap. He poured two glasses and slid one toward me across the marble.

I picked it up. Held it by the stem, the way my dad had taught me when I was fourteen — "hold it here, not the bowl, the heat from your hand changes the temperature" — and took a sip. It was good. Round, warm, the kind of red that tastes like it was made by someone who cared about dirt.

Wade was watching me. Not obviously — he was still cooking, still chopping, still managing two pans. But I caught him. A side-glance. Quick. Cataloging something.

"You hold wine like you've done it before," he said. Casual. Almost throwaway.

"My parents drink wine."

"Most parents drink wine. Most of their kids don't hold the glass by the stem."

I shrugged. "My dad's particular about it."

He let it go. Turned back to the stove. But I'd felt the pressure of the observation — light, precise, the way you test a joint for weakness by pressing gently and seeing what moves.

---

We ate on the terrace. The sun was gone but the sky was still holding color — deep blue bleeding into orange at the horizon, the ocean dark and flat below us. Wade had strung small lights along the railing. They cast a warm, low glow that made everything feel closer than it was.

The pad thai was good. Actually good. The noodles were right, the peanuts were crushed instead of whole, and the lime was fresh-squeezed. He ate fast, the way athletes eat — fork moving with purpose, no pausing between bites.

"So," he said, leaning back in his chair. "Justin from West Valley. Senior year. Swimmer. AP Lit student. No Instagram presence. Sends DMs to strangers about Swedish furniture. What's the real story?"

"That is the real story."

"That's the résumé. I'm asking about the story."

I took a sip of wine. "There's not much of one. School, swim, friends. That's basically it."

"Bullshit."

I looked at him. He was watching me the way he'd watched me hold the wine glass — steady, unhurried, the evaluation happening behind his eyes rather than in his words.

"Everyone has a story," he said. "The people who say they don't are usually sitting on the most interesting one."

"Or they're just boring."

"You're not boring. I've been talking to you for five days. Boring people don't keep me up until four in the morning. Boring people don't tell me to post my outtakes. Boring people don't drive to Pacific Palisades on a Friday night to eat pad thai with someone they met on Instagram."

I didn't have an answer for that. He was right — every point, clean and direct, like bullets hitting a target in sequence. I took another sip of wine instead.

He smiled. "It's okay. You don't have to tell me. I'm just noting the gap."

"What gap?"

"Between who you say you are and who you actually seem to be. There's a gap. It's fine. Everyone's got one."

He said it so easily. Like pointing out that the sky was a particular color — observational, no weight attached. He wasn't accusing me of anything. He was just letting me know he could see the edges of the mask, and he'd decided not to pull it off.

---

After dinner we moved to the living room. The couch was deep — the kind that you sink into and don't want to leave. We sat at opposite ends at first, the gap between us filled with wine glasses and the low sound of music Wade had put on. Something ambient. No lyrics. Just texture.

He talked about content. About the gap between what he showed the camera and what he actually felt while making it. About how performing authenticity was still performing, and he hadn't figured out where the line was.

I talked about swimming. About the way competition breaks you down to a single number — your time — and how that simplicity was either the best or the worst thing about the sport depending on the day.

We talked about mothers. Chinese mothers specifically — the weight of expectation expressed through posture, the disappointment communicated through silence, the love that was real but arrived in packaging that looked like control.

The gap on the couch had been shrinking. Not intentionally — or maybe intentionally, but in the way that bodies decide things before brains sign off. We'd each shifted toward the center, inch by inch, repositioning legs, adjusting angles, until we were close enough that I could feel the heat from his shoulder without touching it.

Wade set his wine glass down on the floor. Turned to face me. His knee touched mine. He didn't move it.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"Yeah."

He looked at me. A long look. A beat and a half too long — the kind of silence where the silence itself becomes the content, and you both know it.

"You're not who you say you are, are you?"

My chest tightened. Not panic — something more like the feeling before a race starts. The stillness before motion. The held breath.

He smiled. One side of his mouth. The same half-smile from the front door.

"It's fine," he said. "I don't care."
