# Chapter 28: Ice Pack
*(POV: Justin)*

The orange line train had that specific smell of brake dust and warm vinyl that usually made me zone out after seventh period. Today it just made my stomach roll.

I sat near the center doors with my back against the scratched plastic partition, keeping my right hand tucked deep inside the pocket of my oversized hoodie. Every time the train hit a switch point near North Hollywood, the jolt sent a sharp, hot throb from my second knuckle straight up into my forearm. The skin felt tight, like someone had wrapped a thick rubber band around the joint and pumped warm water under the surface.

In the reflection of the double-paned train window, I looked like an extra from a low-budget zombie movie. Dark gray smudges under my eyes, hair sticking up on one side where I had rubbed my scalp in Vice Principal Davies's office, and a mouth pulled into a flat, ugly line.

Folded in my back pocket was the official carbon-copy suspension slip. Three school days. Return date: Friday morning, eight o'clock, with a parent or legal guardian for a reinstatement conference.

I kept my eyes on the floor between my sneakers. Every time the train slowed down, I heard Andrew's voice again. It was stuck in my head on a loop, quiet and clear, cutting through the rattle of the tracks.

*Marcus isn't from Westbrook. I asked him for your number because he minds his own business.*

I had walked away from the east gate because if I stayed there another ten seconds, I would have thrown up in the ivy next to the bike racks. Andrew hadn't shouted. He hadn't even looked angry. He had just delivered the facts with that same terrifying precision he used for AP History notes, peeling away my entire theory like old scotch tape.

When the train pulled into my station, I stepped onto the platform and walked the four blocks to our apartment building without looking up once. The afternoon air in the valley was dry and cool, smelling like exhaust from the boulevard and somebody's laundry vent.

I climbed the outdoor concrete stairs to unit 204. My key felt clumsy in my left hand. I had to brace my right wrist against my ribcage just to turn the deadbolt without screaming.

Inside, the apartment was completely silent.

The vertical blinds in the living room were half-drawn, throwing thin yellow stripes across the brown linoleum floor. The place smelled of lemon floor cleaner and yesterday's reheated coffee. On the front of the refrigerator, held up by an old magnetized pizza coupon, was a lined index card written in my mom's quick, rounded cursive:

*Leftover pollo and rice in the blue container. Eat before seven. Working the dinner shift at the clinic, back by 11:30. Lock the deadbolt.*

Next to the microwave, the red LED light on our landline answering machine blinked in a slow, rhythmic pulse. One new message.

I did not press play. I did not need to hear the automated voice of the school attendance office or Davies's dry administrative tone explaining that Justin Herrera had been involved in a physical altercation during fifth-period lunch. My mom would hear it at midnight when she dropped her purse on the counter. I would deal with that when the clock ran out.

I walked over to the freezer, yanked open the door with my left hand, and pulled out a half-empty bag of frozen sweet peas. I grabbed a waffle-weave dishtowel from the oven handle, wrapped the frozen bag in two layers of cotton, and carried it into my bedroom.

I dropped onto my unmade bed, kicked off my sneakers, and slumped back against the pillows.

When I pressed the cold pack against the swollen ridge of my knuckles, the pain was so sharp my breath hitched in my throat. I squeezed my eyes shut and pressed harder. The cold dug in, burning before it started to numb the throbbing pulse in my bone.

My phone vibrated against my hip.

It had been vibrating every thirty seconds since I left the main office. I pulled it out with my left hand and tapped the screen.

The notification tray was completely flooded.

Dylan had sent nineteen messages to our four-person group chat:
*dude are you alive*
*did you actually break marcus jaw*
*reese said there was blood on the floor*
*why the fuck did you do that*
*justin answer your fucking phone*

Tyler had texted twice in private:
*You left your history binder under table four. Reeves took it.*
*Let me know if you need ice or whatever.*

There were seven direct messages on Instagram from people I barely knew. Sophomore track runners, guys from fourth-period chemistry, some girl named Chloe who sat two rows behind me in freshman year. Everyone wanted to know what happened. Everyone had a version of the story. Someone posted on their story that Marcus called me a slur. Someone else claimed I caught Marcus stealing my car keys.

At the bottom of the stack was a single iMessage from Samira, sent forty-five minutes ago:
*Are you at home?*

I stared at the glowing rectangle until the screen timed out and went black. I flipped the phone facedown on the quilt.

In the dark room, the silence was louder than the cafeteria had been.

I closed my eyes and the image hit me instantly: Marcus sitting on the gray plastic chair, turning around with a half-eaten sandwich in his hand. His face completely open, relaxed, not expecting a single thing. He had not even put his arm up to block. When my knuckles caught the corner of his jaw, the sound had been dull, like dropping a hardback book on carpet. His head had snapped sideways, his shoulder catching the edge of the lunch tray, sending plastic forks and tater tots scattering across the floor.

He had looked up at me from the tile with his eyes wide, totally blank, trying to calculate what had just happened.

And I had stood over him with my fist shaking, breathing through my teeth, convinced that I was punishing a traitor.

I pulled the dishtowel away from my hand. The peas had started to thaw into mushy lumps. The skin over my knuckles was bright crimson, mottled with dark blue bruising where the bone had struck bone.

The worst part was the math.

I had built a whole fortress out of bad deductions. I had watched Marcus get weird in October, watched him look at Andrew in the halls, watched the group go quiet whenever Andrew's name came up. I had taken Andrew's silver bracelet, the mysterious partner from Westbrook, the private phone calls, the second phone in the penthouse, and I had glued Marcus's face right onto the middle of it.

I thought Marcus had gone behind my back. I thought they were both laughing at me, the private school kid and the smart-ass friend, treating me like the dumb project kid who was only good for a few weeks of distraction.

And none of it was real.

Andrew's partner was some swimmer named Zack who lived on the other side of the 405. Andrew had never looked at Marcus twice. Marcus had only asked for my number because Andrew asked him to, and Marcus had kept his mouth shut because Marcus never broke a promise.

I had thrown a punch in front of two hundred people because I was humiliated, because Andrew told me he had someone else, and because I needed someone to hurt as much as I did.

At six-fifteen, three heavy knocks landed on the front door.

I didn't move. I lay frozen on the bed, staring at the ceiling fan.

The knocking came again, louder, accompanied by a flat voice through the hollow wood.

"Justin. Open the door before Mrs. Alvarez comes out with her broom."

Samira.

I exhaled slowly, sat up, and dragged my feet across the linoleum. I unlocked the deadbolt and pulled the door open three inches.

Samira stood on the landing under the yellow bug light. She was wearing her oversized denim jacket with the sleeves rolled up, carrying my heavy canvas backpack in her left hand. Her expression was neutral, which was always the most dangerous way Samira looked.

"Can I come in, or are you going to hide behind the door like a child?" she asked.

I stepped back, opening the door wider.

She walked past me into the kitchen, dropped my backpack onto one of the vinyl dining chairs, and set a plastic convenience store bag on the counter. She turned around and took one look at my right hand, which was still clutching the damp towel.

"Nice wrap job," she said.

"It's peas," I muttered.

"Sit down."

I pulled out the opposite chair and sat down. My shoulders felt heavy, like someone had loaded twenty pounds of gravel into my hoodie.

Samira pulled a bottle of generic ibuprofen and two bottles of cold water out of the convenience store bag. She slid one water bottle and two red pills across the laminate table toward me.

"Take those before your wrist swells up to the size of a grapefruit," she said, sitting down across from me.

I looked at the pills, then picked them up with my left hand and swallowed them with a gulp of water. The plastic crinkled loudly in the quiet room.

"Dylan is losing his mind," Samira said evenly. "He thinks you joined a gang or had a psychotic break. Tyler is just confused because you don't even like fighting."

"Did Marcus go home?" I asked. My voice sounded thin, like dry paper.

"Eventually," Samira said. She leaned her elbows on the table and looked at me with those dark, unblinking eyes. "First he went to urgent care on Sherman Way. His mom had to leave work."

A cold knot twisted in my gut. "Is it broken?"

"Bruised bone. Soft tissue contusion. He's got three stitches inside his lower lip where his tooth cut the cheek."

I pressed the frozen peas harder against my knuckles, wishing the ice would just freeze my whole hand until I couldn't feel it. "Shit."

"Yeah. Shit."

"Did... did Davies call the police?" I asked, looking down at the tabletop.

Samira let out a dry, humorless breath. "They were about to. The campus resource officer was in the office when they brought Marcus in with the ice pack. Davies had the incident report on the desk. Zero-tolerance policy means an automatic referral to the district board for battery."

I looked up at her, my heart thumping against my ribs. "Why didn't they?"

"Because Marcus told them he fell."

The words hung in the air between us, heavy and impossible.

"What?"

"He told Davies he slipped near the tray return," Samira said, her voice dropping into a quiet, lethal register. "He said someone spilled chocolate milk on the linoleum, he lost his footing, and his chin hit the metal corner of the recycling bin. He said you were reaching out to grab his jacket to keep him from falling."

My mouth opened, but nothing came out. My throat felt like it was closing up.

"He looked Davies right in the eye with blood on his shirt and told that ridiculous lie," Samira continued. "Davies didn't believe him for a second, but without the victim pressing charges or confirming a fight, they couldn't write it up as battery. That's the only reason you got three days for disorderly conduct instead of being expelled and sent to continuation school."

I stared at the salt shaker on the table. My eyes burned, hot and stinging, but I refused to let tears spill over.

"Why would he do that?" I whispered. "I hit him in the face, Samira. In front of everybody."

"Because he's Marcus," she said simply. "He's an idiot who thinks loyalty means taking a hit and keeping quiet. Which makes you look about ten times worse for doing what you did."

"I thought..." I started, but the words felt clumsy and pathetic before they even left my mouth. "I thought him and Andrew..."

"I know what you thought," Samira cut in quietly.

I looked at her, startled.

"You think nobody has eyes?" she said. "You've been walking around like a raw nerve since Thanksgiving. Every time Andrew walked past our table, you looked like you were going to throw up or jump out a window. And Marcus was acting weird because he was watching you spiral, and Marcus doesn't know how to handle people who don't make sense."

She paused, watching my face.

"Marcus didn't touch Andrew, Justin. Andrew doesn't even talk to Marcus in Government. He barely talks to anyone."

"Andrew told me," I said, my voice barely audible. "At the east gate. Before I left."

"Good. Then you know how completely stupid you were."

Samira stood up, scraping her chair legs against the floor. She grabbed her purse and slung the strap over her shoulder. She walked toward the front door, then stopped with her hand on the brass knob.

She didn't look angry. She just looked tired, the way people look when they have to clean up a mess they didn't make.

"I brought your binder and your homework assignments for English and History," she said, nodding toward my backpack. "Reeves said you can submit the project slides via Google Classroom by Thursday night. He didn't ask questions."

"Thanks," I mumbled.

"Don't thank me," Samira said. "And don't call Marcus tonight. He's at home with an ice bag on his face and his mom hovering over him."

"Is he going to talk to me?"

Samira opened the door. The cold evening air swept into the hallway, carrying the distant sound of traffic on the avenue.

"He'll talk to you when you figure out how to say you're sorry without making it an excuse about Andrew Hayes," she said.

She walked out and pulled the door shut behind her. The latch clicked into place with a small, final sound.

I stood there in the middle of the kitchen for a long time.

The hum of the refrigerator kicked on, vibrating through the linoleum floor. Across the room, the red light on the answering machine was still blinking, patient and steady in the dim light.

I picked up my phone with my left hand, unlocked the screen, and opened Marcus's contact card.

The screen glowed blue against the dark counter. His name sat there at the top: *Marcus W.*

I had three whole days in this empty apartment before I was allowed to set foot on campus again. Three days with my swollen knuckles, my mom's unread voicemail, and the exact truth of what I had done, with nowhere left to run.
