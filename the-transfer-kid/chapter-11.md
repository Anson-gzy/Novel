# Chapter 11: Not You, Not Him

*(POV: Marcus)*
I ran out of patience on a Tuesday.

I don't know why it was a Tuesday specifically. There was nothing special about it — no event, no trigger, no conversation that pushed me past a threshold I'd been carefully maintaining for weeks. I just woke up that morning and the restraint was gone, used up overnight like a battery that had been draining without me noticing.

What I'd been doing — the watching, the cataloguing, the careful distance I'd maintained between what I felt and what I did about it — all of it had been powered by a specific belief: that if I just kept observing, the situation would explain itself. That Andrew's behavior would eventually resolve into a pattern I could understand and file away. That the pull I felt would fade if I gave it enough time and enough silence.

It hadn't faded. It had gotten worse. And the reason it had gotten worse was because I kept replaying the contrast.

The way Andrew had been with Justin in those early weeks. I wasn't there for all of it — the penthouse visit, the phone call, the playlist — but I'd seen the edges. I'd seen Andrew light up in the hallway when Justin walked by, the subtle shift in his posture, the way his attention locked and held. I'd seen Andrew ask for Justin's number with a specificity that revealed planning, maybe even nervousness. I'd seen Andrew do things that required effort — real effort, the kind you don't spend on someone you don't care about.

And then there was me. The person Andrew looked through. The one who got the functional version in class and the absolute nothing everywhere else. The one who'd been sitting three feet away four days a week for over a month and had received approximately zero evidence that Andrew knew his first name without being reminded.

The contrast did something to my thinking. It made me start asking questions I hadn't wanted to ask.

Was Andrew's interest in Justin ever just friendly? The intensity of it — the phone call, the number request, the way he'd basically built a bridge to Justin's life piece by piece — was that how friends acted? Was that how someone who was casually interested in hanging out behaved?

Or was it something else?

And if it was something else — if Andrew's attention toward Justin had been more than it appeared — then what did his indifference toward me mean? Was it just personality? Was it just Andrew being Andrew, rationing his warmth to the people he'd chosen and ignoring the rest?

Or did it mean something specific about what Andrew did and didn't want?

I didn't like these questions. They felt invasive, presumptuous, like I was building a case on circumstantial evidence and personal frustration. But I couldn't stop turning them over, and the fact that I couldn't stop told me something I wasn't ready to hear.

---

I started gathering. Quietly. Not dramatically — I wasn't going to become the person who interrogated people about another person's social life. But I paid attention in the spaces where information moved naturally.

In Government, I watched who Andrew talked to. He didn't talk to many people, but the ones he did — Mia from the hallway, a guy named Jordan who sat behind him, occasionally Okoro after class — he treated with a specific caliber of engagement. Not warm, exactly. Functional-plus. Like he'd decided they were worth a few degrees above room temperature.

I asked Dylan, casually, if he'd seen Andrew around lately. "Not really," Dylan said. "I think he's been keeping to himself. Why?"

"No reason."

"You keep asking about this guy."

"I keep observing this guy. There's a difference."

"The difference being?"

"Observation is passive. Asking is active. I'm doing the first one."

"You just asked me a question about him."

I changed the subject.

I asked Becca if she'd heard anything on the Instagram network — whether Andrew was hanging out with anyone, whether he'd been seen with someone outside school. Becca, who kept half an ear on the gossip current at all times without ever contributing to it, said she hadn't heard anything specific. "He's pretty off the radar," she said. "His Instagram's been dead. No stories in like three weeks."

I told myself it was general curiosity. A puzzle I was trying to complete. I didn't convince myself, and I didn't try very hard, which was its own kind of honesty.

---

It happened in Government.

A Tuesday in early December. Cold enough that most people were wearing jackets inside, the heaters in the building doing the bare minimum. Okoro had given us the last fifteen minutes of class for independent work on our policy papers, which meant most people were on their phones and Okoro was grading something at his desk with the focused intensity of someone who had given up on surveillance.

Andrew was at his desk. Notebook open, pen moving, the seventy-percent version I'd gotten used to. He hadn't looked at me once all period, which was standard. I was four feet away, which was also standard. The distance between us felt permanent — not because it was large, but because neither of us had made a move to close it.

Until I did.

"Hey."

Andrew looked up. The same half-second of acknowledgment he always gave — eyes on me, input processed, categorized, filed. Ready to move on.

"Can I ask you something?"

A pause. Not long. Just the space it takes to decide whether a question is worth answering before you've heard it.

"Sure."

I didn't ease into it. I don't do that. I said what I'd been thinking for weeks, in the plainest language I had.

"What's your problem with me?"

Andrew's pen stopped. He looked at me — really looked, for the first time since the day he'd walked into this classroom and given me that nod of mutual recognition. His expression didn't change, exactly, but something shifted behind it. A recalibration. Like he was adjusting his assessment of me in real time.

"I don't have a problem with you," he said.

"You do. You talk to me in class like I exist and then walk past me in the hallway like I'm part of the wall. You can hold a conversation with half the school — I've watched you do it — and then go completely blank every time I'm standing right there."

Silence. Andrew set his pen down. He didn't look uncomfortable — that wasn't the right word. He looked like someone who'd been handed a problem he hadn't expected and was processing it faster than his face could keep up with.

"I didn't realize I was doing that," he said.

"You were doing it."

"Okay. I'm sorry if —"

"I'm not asking for an apology. I'm asking what the problem is."

Another pause. Longer this time. The noise of the classroom — keyboards clicking, someone whispering two rows back, Okoro's pen scratching — filled the space between us.

Andrew looked at his hands. Then at me. When he spoke, his voice was the same as it always was — flat, measured, no decoration — but there was a quality underneath it that I hadn't heard before. Something close to tired.

"Marcus. Stop."

"Stop what?"

"Whatever this is. The watching. The questions. The thing where you walk past my locker and say hey and then spend the rest of the day analyzing my response." He said it without malice. Without performance. Like he was reading a weather report. "I'm not interested."

The word landed in the center of my chest and sat there.

"Interested in what?"

"In whatever you're building toward. I'm not — I can see what's happening. And I'm telling you, plainly, that it's not going to happen."

I didn't say anything. I sat there and let the sentence settle.

"It's not about you," Andrew continued. "It's not about anything you did or didn't do. I have a partner. That's it. That's the entire reason."

"A partner."

"Someone I'm with. Not here — back at my old school. We've been together for a while." He said it the way he said everything: factually. Like he was closing a door that was never really open, and he wanted to make sure I understood that it was never really open so I wouldn't waste time looking for a handle.

"Is that why you and Justin —" I started, and then stopped, because the look on his face told me I'd reached the edge of what he was willing to discuss.

"Justin is a different situation," he said. Quiet. Final. "And it's not something I'm going to talk about."

"Okay."

"I'm not trying to be cruel."

"I know."

"I just don't want you to spend time on something that doesn't have anywhere to go."

I nodded. Once. And then I turned back to my notebook, and the fifteen minutes of independent work continued, and Okoro never looked up from his grading, and nobody in the room knew that anything had happened at all.

---

The walk to the bus stop was cold.

Not metaphorically — literally cold. December in LA doesn't get brutal, but the wind was coming off the hills and I hadn't brought a jacket heavy enough for it. I walked with my hands in my pockets and my jaw tight and I didn't think about anything in particular, which was a lie I told myself and almost believed.

He had a partner.

The sentence kept replaying. Not the content — I'd processed the content immediately, the way I process everything: quickly, cleanly, without drama. He had a partner. Someone from his old school. He wasn't interested in me. He wasn't interested in Justin either. The door was closed. Sealed. Locked. Whatever metaphor you wanted.

I understood it. I accepted it. There was nothing complicated about the information itself.

What I couldn't accept, exactly, was the thing that deflated in my chest when he said it. Something I hadn't realized was still inflated — some pocket of air, some held breath, some tiny pressurized space where a possibility had been living without my permission.

It was gone now. And the absence of it felt like something.

Not heartbreak. That's too big a word and too dramatic a concept for what this was. More like a settling. Like a building that had been leaning slightly, so slightly you didn't notice until it corrected itself, and now the new angle felt wrong even though it was the right one.

I didn't tell anyone. Not Dylan, who would have made it a thing. Not Samira, who would have understood but whose understanding would have made it more real. Not Becca, who would have been kind in a way that would have made it worse. Not Tyler, who would have said something accidentally perfect and accidentally devastating in the same sentence.

Definitely not Justin.

I sat with it. In my room that night, doing homework I wasn't really doing, the Government textbook open to a page I'd read three times without absorbing a word. I sat with the deflated thing in my chest and let it be what it was.

I was fine. I'd been fine before — after other things, smaller things, things that didn't have names or faces attached to them. I'd be fine again. That was the thing about being someone who didn't perform: when the feeling came, you sat with it alone, and when it passed, nobody knew it had been there.

It would pass.

I closed the textbook and turned off the light and lay in the dark and waited for it to start.
