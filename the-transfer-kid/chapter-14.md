# Chapter 14: One Day, One Night

*(POV: Zack)*
The first hour was quiet.

Not the good kind of quiet — the kind where two people are comfortable enough to let silence exist between them. This was the other kind. The kind where silence is the only thing left after everything loud has finished happening, and neither of you knows whether more loud things are coming.

We sat against the wall. Andrew's shoulder pressed against mine, not because we'd chosen to sit that close but because the space with breathable air was narrow. The emergency lights had died maybe twenty minutes after the last big sound, and now the only illumination was the screen of Andrew's phone, which he turned on every few minutes to check for signal. No signal. The screen lit his face in cold blue and then went dark again, and each time it did, the darkness felt thicker than before.

My leg was bad. I knew that without looking at it, the way you know something is broken before anyone tells you — a deep, specific wrongness that radiated from below my knee into my hip every time I shifted. I'd stopped shifting. I'd found a position where the pain was a low, constant hum instead of a sharp spike, and I was holding it with the focus of someone balancing something fragile on a shelf.

Andrew was next to me. He hadn't spoken since the phone call. He was just sitting there, breathing, his hands resting on his knees with the dust still caked into the lines of his palms. Every few minutes he'd turn his head slightly — listening for something. Rescue, maybe. Or more collapse. Or just the sounds of a building settling into whatever shape it was going to be for the rest of the night.

At some point — I don't know when, because time had stopped working in any useful way — he said:

"What's your name?"

I almost laughed. We'd been shoulder to shoulder for an hour and we hadn't even done that.

"Zack," I said.

"Zack what?"

"Chen."

A pause.

"Andrew," he said, which was unnecessary because I already knew, but I understood the gesture. We were starting from the beginning. Whatever we'd been at Westbrook — strangers, specifically, the kind who share a building but not a life — that was done. We were something else now. Something that didn't have a name yet.

"I know," I said. "We go to the same school."

Another pause.

"Went," he said. Quiet. Like a correction he was making for himself, not for me.

---

The talking started slowly.

Andrew asked about my leg. I told him what I knew, which wasn't much — something below the knee was wrong, and I couldn't put weight on it, and I was trying not to think about it because thinking about it made the pain worse. He nodded like that was a reasonable strategy. Then he asked if I was cold. I was. The building had lost heat when the power went, and the June evening outside the walls was doing nothing to keep the temperature up inside this corner of crumbled plaster and exposed steel.

"Here," he said, and took off his jacket. The tuxedo jacket, torn at the shoulder, covered in dust. He draped it over my legs — not my shoulders, my legs, like he'd calculated that the injured part needed warmth more than the rest of me. It was a small thing, but it was also the most deliberate act of care anyone had performed in my direction in the last two hours, and I felt it land somewhere in my chest with a weight that didn't match the gesture.

"Thanks," I said.

"Don't mention it."

We were quiet again. Then I asked him something — I don't remember what. Something about school, maybe. Something small and normal and completely irrelevant to the situation, which was the point. Because the situation was a collapsed building and smoke and my leg and his parents and the dark, and if we talked about any of that we'd have to feel all of it, and neither of us was ready for that.

So we talked about other things.

Andrew talked about Westbrook. Not the version you'd hear in a brochure — the manicured campus, the college placement rates, the carefully curated image of excellence. He talked about the texture of it. The specific quality of being a student that nobody wanted around.

"It's not like they were cruel," he said. His voice was even. Almost clinical. Like he was describing a case study he'd read about in someone else's file. "Nobody bullied me. Nobody went out of their way to exclude me. It was more like — I just didn't register. I'd walk into a room and the room wouldn't change. You know how when someone walks in, there's a shift? Even if nobody says anything, the energy moves. People adjust."

"Yeah."

"That didn't happen with me. I'd walk in and the room stayed exactly the same. Like I was made of the same material as the walls."

I listened. I was good at listening — always had been. My mother said it was my best quality. My father said it would make me a good lawyer, which was his way of saying the same thing in a language he understood better.

"I used to sit in the cafeteria," Andrew continued. "Not at a table — I didn't have a regular table. I'd sit wherever there was space. And people would be talking, and I'd be there, and they'd keep talking like I wasn't. Not because they were ignoring me on purpose. Because they genuinely didn't notice. I'd been so quiet for so long that I'd become part of the background noise."

He said all of this without self-pity. That was the thing that struck me — the complete absence of performance. He wasn't telling me a sad story and waiting for my reaction. He was reporting. Cataloguing. Saying things out loud that he might never have said before, and the dark and the dust and the waiting made the words come out straighter than they would have in daylight.

"That sounds terrible," I said.

"It wasn't terrible. It was just — what it was. Terrible implies someone is doing something to you. Nobody was doing anything. That was the whole problem."

---

I talked too. More than I normally would.

Something about the dark made honesty feel lower-risk than usual. Like the words I was saying existed only inside this small pocket of breathable air between us, and once we left, they'd dissipate like the dust.

I told him about swim team. About the specific pressure of being good at something your parents chose for you before you were old enough to choose for yourself. About the hours in the pool, the chlorine smell that never fully left my skin, the way my father recorded every meet on his phone and watched the footage afterward like a coach reviewing game tape.

"Do you like swimming?" Andrew asked.

"I'm good at it."

"That's not what I asked."

I thought about it. Really thought about it, in a way I hadn't in years. "I don't know," I said. "I've been doing it since I was six. At some point it stopped being something I liked or didn't like and became just — what I do. Like breathing. You don't evaluate whether you enjoy breathing."

"You could stop."

"My father would have opinions about that."

"Everyone's parents have opinions. Not all of them matter."

He said it so plainly that it took me a second to register what he'd said. And then I remembered where we were, and who his parents were — the two adults I'd seen across the ballroom, the ones the brother had said were fine — and I felt something sharp and ugly move through me.

We went quiet for a while.

---

The night got colder. At some point I started shaking — not from fear, from temperature and probably shock, my body finally catching up to everything that had happened. Andrew moved closer without saying anything. We sat with no space between us, his arm against mine, and the shared warmth was marginal but real.

We went through topics that strangers don't normally reach. It happened gradually, without either of us marking the transitions. One minute we were talking about school and the next we were talking about what we were afraid of.

"Failing," Andrew said when I asked. "Not academically. Just — being someone who doesn't amount to anything. Walking through a whole life the way I walked through Westbrook. Present but irrelevant."

"Is that why you work so hard?"

"Maybe. Or maybe I just don't know how to stop."

I told him mine. "Disappointing my parents. Being the version of their son that doesn't match the plan."

"What's the plan?"

"Stanford. Law school. Something respectable. The kind of life you can describe at a dinner party without anyone changing the subject."

"Do you want that?"

"Parts of it."

"Which parts?"

"I don't know. I haven't figured that out yet."

He nodded. Not judgmental. Not sympathetic. Just acknowledging — like he understood that some answers take longer than others and he was willing to wait.

We talked about what we thought our lives would look like. Andrew said he'd always assumed he'd end up alone — not lonely, just singular. A person who moved through the world efficiently without needing to anchor to anyone else. He said it the same way he said everything: flat, measured, without asking me to feel anything about it.

I said I'd always assumed the opposite — that my life would look like my parents' life. Organized. Paired. A house, a career, a structure that held everything in place. I said I was starting to wonder if that assumption was a plan or a cage.

"It depends on whether you chose it," Andrew said.

"I didn't choose it."

"Then you know which one it is."

---

At some point — not dramatically, not announced — I realized something.

It wasn't a big moment. There was no shift in the air, no change in the light, no sentence that acted as a trigger. I was just sitting there, in the dark, with a person I'd never spoken to before tonight, and I realized that the thing I was feeling wasn't just the adrenaline of shared survival or the strange intimacy of being trapped. It was something simpler and harder to dismiss.

I didn't want this person to disappear once we got out.

Not in an intense way. Not in a way that had a label or an expectation attached to it. Just — the fact that Andrew Hayes existed, and that I now knew what he sounded like when he was honest, and that I'd seen the inside of his head in a way that nobody at Westbrook ever had — I didn't want to let that go. I didn't want to walk out of this building and go back to being strangers who shared a peripheral awareness of each other's existence.

I didn't say it. There was no version of that sentence that would survive contact with daylight. It was a thought that belonged to this specific darkness, and I let it stay there.

Andrew's breathing had slowed. Not sleep — I don't think either of us slept the entire time. Just a deeper rhythm, the kind your body defaults to when it's been running on adrenaline for too long and the fuel starts to run out.

"Zack," he said.

"Yeah."

"Thank you. For getting me out."

I looked at the spot where the column had fallen. I couldn't see it in the dark, but I knew where it was.

"Don't mention it," I said, echoing his own words back without planning to.

He breathed out — that quiet exhale, the kind that means something but doesn't require a response.

We sat there. We waited.

---

They found us eighteen hours after the building went quiet.

The rescue was loud and fast and came from a direction I hadn't expected — through a wall, not a door. I heard machines first, then voices, then light so bright it hurt. Flashlights. Headlamps. The orange glow of high-visibility vests. Someone was shouting instructions in a voice trained to carry through chaos.

Hands reached in. Multiple people, moving with the practiced efficiency of a process they'd rehearsed a hundred times and performed for real more often than they wanted. Someone put a brace on my leg and the pain went from a hum to a scream and I gripped the edge of whatever I was lying on and made a sound I wasn't proud of.

Andrew was next to me for another few seconds — I saw his face in the light, the dust, the steadiness in his eyes that hadn't changed from the moment I'd first looked at him under the fallen column. Then people moved between us. Medical teams, firemen, the machinery of aftermath.

Someone was asking me questions. Name. Age. Allergies. I answered on autopilot while trying to keep Andrew in my line of sight. He was being moved on a stretcher, two people on each side, and he was looking back at me over his shoulder.

Then — just before they took him through the opening they'd cut in the wall — he reached out. Not dramatically. Not with a speech or a look that was meant to convey everything we hadn't said. He just extended his hand toward me, and I took it, and his grip was the same as it had been when I pulled him out from under the column. Tight. Specific. Like he was holding on to something he wasn't ready to let go of.

Then they moved him through the wall and our hands separated and he was gone.

---

The hospital was white and cold and smelled like chemicals that existed specifically to cover up the smell of other chemicals.

I was on a gurney, then in a room, then being examined by people who talked about me in the third person while I was right there. My leg was discussed in terms I mostly understood — fracture, displacement, surgical intervention. Someone put an IV in my hand. Someone else asked me to rate my pain on a scale of one to ten and I said seven because saying ten felt like admitting something I wasn't ready to admit.

My parents were found. My mother arrived at the hospital forty minutes after I did — I know because the clock on the wall was the only thing I had to look at. She was crying in a controlled way, the kind of crying that happens behind a composed face. My father was behind her. He put his hand on my shoulder and squeezed once, hard, and didn't say anything, which from my father meant everything.

They'd gotten out through a service exit on the west side. They'd spent hours looking for me. The relief on their faces was so raw that I had to look away.

Then, later — when my parents had gone to talk to the surgeon and I was alone in the pre-op area, waiting for the anesthesia that would put me under for however long it took to fix whatever was wrong with my leg — I heard something.

Two nurses. Or a nurse and a doctor. Standing on the other side of the curtain, talking in the low, professional voices that hospital staff use when they're discussing information that belongs to someone else.

I heard two names. A man's name and a woman's name. I didn't catch the last name, but I caught enough — the gala, the east section, the structural collapse, the recovery timeline.

And then one of them said: "Pronounced at the scene."

I lay on the gurney and stared at the ceiling tiles.

Pronounced at the scene. The east section. The same section where I'd found Andrew pinned under a column. The same section where two adults had been standing near a table on the left side, one holding a glass of wine with both hands, one tall with military posture.

Andrew's parents.

Andrew, who was somewhere in this hospital or another one, who had called his brother from a cracked phone in the dark, who had asked if his parents were okay and had been told they were fine. Who had exhaled like someone letting go of something too heavy to carry and had said "okay" twice and had believed it because he needed to believe it because the alternative was not survivable in a collapsed building with no way out.

The anesthesia came. I felt it in my hand first — cold, spreading up my arm. The ceiling tiles went soft. My thoughts went slow.

The last thing I thought, before everything went quiet, was about Andrew finding out. About the moment when the brother's controlled voice would have to say the thing it had chosen not to say on the phone. About Andrew's face when the truth arrived — the real truth, the one that had been waiting behind the careful words.

I couldn't do anything about it. I was in a hospital bed with a broken leg and a needle in my arm and the darkness was coming and it was the wrong kind of darkness — chemical, involuntary, nothing like the darkness in the building where Andrew and I had sat shoulder to shoulder and talked about our lives like the words mattered.

The anesthesia took me under. The last thing I saw was the clock on the wall. The last thing I felt was the phantom grip of Andrew's hand in mine.

Then nothing.
