# Chapter 18: The Gift

I bought the book on a Wednesday.

It was a Wednesday because I had a free period after lunch, and because the bookstore on Fairfax was between school and the train, and because I'd been carrying the decision around for three days and the weight of it was starting to interfere with my ability to think about anything else.

The book was a specific edition of a specific novel that Andrew had mentioned exactly once, in passing, during a conversation in his room about six weeks earlier. We'd been talking about the show we were watching — something about a character making a choice that didn't make logical sense — and Andrew had said, almost to himself: "It's like that book. The one where the guy walks across the whole country because he thinks he can save someone by walking." He'd said the title and the author's name and then moved on, and I'd filed it away the way I filed everything about Andrew — unconsciously, automatically, in the part of my brain that had been running a continuous backup of every word he said since September.

The bookstore had two copies. I bought the one with the better cover — hardback, dark blue, the kind of book that felt like it was worth keeping. The woman at the register asked if I wanted it wrapped. I said no. Then I said yes. Then I said no again, and she gave me a look that suggested she'd seen teenagers in this specific state of indecision before, and I took the book in a paper bag and left.

I didn't let myself think too hard about what the gesture meant. I knew what it meant. Buying a book for someone because they mentioned it once, weeks ago, in a sentence that lasted three seconds — that's not casual. That's not what friends do. Friends text you a link and say "you should check this out." They don't go to a bookstore on a Wednesday afternoon and stand at the register having a crisis about gift wrapping.

But I'd spent weeks not saying things. Weeks watching Andrew's attention shift and his phone go face-up and his warmth drop by degrees. Weeks running calculations in my head that produced answers I didn't want. The book was not a solution. It was more like a test — a way of putting something in front of Andrew and watching his reaction and seeing whether the warmth I remembered was still in there somewhere, underneath the distance.

I texted him that afternoon.

*Hey. You free tonight?*

The response came in four minutes. *Yeah. What's up?*

*I got you something. Can I come by?*

A pause. Longer than four minutes. I watched the screen. The typing indicator appeared. Disappeared. Appeared again.

*Yeah. Come over around 7.*

---

The train ride to Andrew's was twenty-two minutes. I'd done it enough times now that the route had become automatic — Red Line, transfer, walk three blocks east, the building with the doorman. The first time I'd made this trip, back in September, I'd been so nervous that I'd changed my shirt twice and sat on my bed for twenty minutes before leaving. Now the nervousness was different. Not anticipatory — more like the low hum of someone who knows something important is about to happen and can't predict which direction it will go.

The book was in my backpack. I'd put it in the paper bag and then put the paper bag in my backpack and then zipped the backpack and then unzipped it to make sure the book was still there, which was irrational behavior that I acknowledged without being able to stop.

Andrew opened the door in a white T-shirt and gray sweatpants. Bare feet. Hair slightly messy, the kind of messy that happened when he ran his hand through it while reading. He looked like someone who'd been home for a while and had settled into the shape of the evening.

"Hey," he said.

"Hey."

He stepped back. I walked in. The apartment was quieter than usual — no laptop on the dining table, no legal pad, no Ethan.

"Ethan's out," Andrew said, reading the question I hadn't asked. "Some work thing. He'll be back late."

"Okay."

I set my backpack by the door. My shoes came off — I'd learned, after the first visit, that Andrew's apartment was a shoes-off household, the kind of detail that feels significant when you're cataloguing someone's life.

We went to his room. The usual configuration — Andrew at his desk, me on the edge of the bed. The window behind him showed the city at the wrong angle for sunset, just the flat, ambient light of early evening reflecting off other buildings.

I pulled the book out of my backpack.

I didn't wrap it. Didn't put it in a gift bag. Just held it out — hardback, dark blue cover, the weight of it solid in my hand.

"Here."

Andrew looked at the book. Then at me. His expression did something I'd never seen it do before — it shifted without his permission. A flicker. Not surprise, exactly. Something closer to recognition. Like he was looking at something he hadn't expected but immediately understood.

He took it. Turned it over. Read the back cover, though I knew he already knew what the book was about. He opened it — not to a specific page, just to the middle, the way some people do with new books, letting the spine crack and the pages fall open.

"You remembered this," he said. Not a question.

"You mentioned it."

"Once. Weeks ago."

"Yeah."

He looked at me. The look lasted longer than it should have — long enough that the air between us changed density, long enough that I had to make a conscious decision not to look away.

"Thank you," he said. And the way he said it — like the words were carrying something heavier than gratitude, like the sentence was a container for something he wasn't saying — did the same thing to my chest that his "I trust you" had done in World History, back when we were still strangers and the most significant thing between us was a group project about the Cold War.

"You're welcome."

He set the book on his desk, carefully, spine facing out. Then he said: "Do you want to get food? There's a place on the next block."

"Yeah. Let's go."

---

The evening was good.

Genuinely good. The kind of good that made me forget, for hours at a time, that something had been wrong. We walked to the restaurant — a small place, Thai, the kind with ten tables and a menu handwritten on a whiteboard. Andrew had been here before; the woman at the counter greeted him by name, which meant he came here enough to be recognized, which meant this was part of his real life, the daily version, not the performance version.

We sat at a table by the window. The conversation was easy — the kind of easy back-and-forth we'd had in the beginning, before the phone glancing and the distance and the weeks of silence. Andrew talked about a class. I talked about Dylan's latest theory about something. We argued about a movie neither of us had actually seen, building increasingly elaborate opinions about something we had no information about, and at one point Andrew almost laughed — not the almost-sound, but something closer to real laughter, and the sound of it hit me like a physical thing.

He was present. Fully present. His phone was in his pocket. His attention was on me — steady, undivided, the way it used to be when we first started hanging out and the world outside his room felt like it was being held at bay by something neither of us had to name.

I let myself relax into it. I stopped running calculations. I stopped tracking his eye movements and his phone and the subtle angles of his attention. I just — was there. With him. In a restaurant with bad lighting and good food and a conversation that felt like coming home to something I'd missed without knowing I'd been gone.

We walked back to his apartment. The night was cold — December cold, not the extreme kind but enough that our breath was visible and our hands went into our pockets. Andrew walked close to me. Close enough that our shoulders touched when we turned corners. Close enough that the distance between us, which had felt like a canyon for weeks, felt like nothing at all.

---

In his room, the dynamic shifted.

Not suddenly. Not with a clear before and after. It was more like the temperature of the evening had been rising gradually all night and now it had reached a threshold, and neither of us marked the moment when casual became something else.

We were sitting on his bed. The show was on — the same one we'd watched that first time, months ago, when I'd come to the penthouse and sat on this same bed and listened to his playlist and felt the world get smaller. Andrew was sitting with his back against the headboard. I was next to him, close enough that I could feel the warmth coming off his arm.

He said something about the show. I said something back. Our eyes met and didn't separate.

What happened next was not a decision I marked with a clear before and after. It wasn't a choice I made at a specific moment — it was more like a current, something that had been flowing underneath every conversation and every silence and every careful, calibrated distance we'd maintained, and now the current surfaced and neither of us stopped it.

His hand was on my arm. My hand was on the side of his face. His eyes closed first. Then mine.

I'm not going to describe what happened next with the detail that a better writer might give it. I don't have the vocabulary and I don't need it. What I can say is that afterward I lay on Andrew's bed and stared at the ceiling and felt calm in a way that was significant — not peaceful, not euphoric, just calm. Like something that had been vibrating inside me for weeks had finally gone still.

Andrew was next to me. On his back, looking at the same ceiling. The room was quiet except for the sounds of the building — the heater, the faint traffic from fourteen floors below, the small mechanical sounds that apartments make when nobody's talking.

I turned my head and looked at him.

This was the moment. I knew it the way you know that a wave is about to break — you can see the shape of it, the gathering force, the specific point where momentum becomes inevitable. I had things I needed to say. About the distance. About the cold. About the weeks of watching his phone and tracking his attention and lying awake at 3 AM running equations that never balanced.

"Andrew."

"Yeah."

"Can I ask you something?"

He didn't answer immediately. His eyes were still on the ceiling, and something in his face changed — a subtle tightening, a contraction around the jaw, the kind of shift you'd miss if you weren't watching closely.

"Yeah," he said.

"What's been going on? The last few weeks. You've been — different."

Silence. Not the comfortable kind. The other kind.

"Different how?" he said. His voice was flat. The flat register. The one he used when he was managing something.

"You know how. The phone. The checking. The —" I gestured at the space between us, which was ironic, given that the space between us was currently nothing. "Something's been off. And I've been trying not to ask about it because I don't want to be the person who needs to know everything. But I need to know this."

Andrew closed his eyes. Not sleepy — deliberate. The kind of closing that means the person is gathering themselves.

I waited.

"I should have told you sooner," he said.

My stomach dropped. Not all the way — halfway. Enough to feel it. Enough to know that whatever came next was going to change the shape of the evening in a way I couldn't undo.

"Told me what?"

He opened his eyes. Looked at the ceiling. Not at me.

"I have a partner," he said.

The sentence hit the air between us and stayed there. Solid. Heavy. Taking up space that used to contain other things — warmth, closeness, the specific quality of attention that Andrew had been giving me all evening, the calm that had settled in my chest thirty seconds ago.

I didn't move for a second. My brain was doing something I couldn't stop — replaying the last three months at triple speed, recontextualizing every interaction, every text, every moment of warmth and every moment of distance through the lens of this single sentence. The bracelet. "Someone who isn't around anymore." The phone face-up. The typing indicator. The gap in the warmth. The way Andrew had pulled me in and then pulled back and then pulled me in again, and the whole time — the whole time —

"A partner," I said.

"Someone from my old school. We've been together since the summer."

Since the summer. Before I met him. Before the first day of school and the principal's office and the group project and the "I trust you" and the penthouse and the playlist and the shirt I changed twice. Before all of it.

"Since the summer," I said.

"Yeah."

"And you didn't think — at any point — in the last three months —"

I stopped. I sat up. Andrew stayed where he was, on his back, looking at the ceiling.

"Justin —"

"Don't." The word came out harder than I intended. Or maybe exactly as hard as I intended. I couldn't tell. The calm was gone. In its place was something that felt like the opposite of calm — not anger, not sadness, something with no name that was pressing against my ribs from the inside.

I got up. Found my shirt. Put it on with the kind of deliberate, automatic movement that happens when your body is executing a sequence while your brain is somewhere else entirely.

"Justin, wait."

"I'm not waiting."

"Can we — can we talk about this?"

"You just told me you have a partner. While I'm in your bed. What is there to talk about?"

He sat up. His face was doing the thing it did when the filters broke — something raw underneath the surface, something that hadn't been processed through the architecture of who he'd decided to be. For a second he looked like someone else entirely. Someone younger. Someone less composed.

"It's complicated," he said.

"It's not complicated. It's simple. You have a partner. You didn't tell me. That's it."

I found my jacket. My backpack. My shoes were by the front door. I walked down the hallway and heard Andrew behind me — bare feet on the hardwood — and I didn't turn around.

"Justin." His voice. Saying my name the way he said everything — measured, specific, calibrated for maximum impact. But the calibration was off tonight. The control was cracking. I could hear the edges of something underneath it, something that might have been the real version of what he was feeling, and I didn't want to hear it because hearing it would make it harder to leave.

I put on my shoes. I didn't tie them properly — just shoved my feet in and pulled the laces tight enough to hold.

"Please," Andrew said. He was standing at the end of the hallway. Silhouetted against the living room windows, the city behind him, the lights of fourteen floors of altitude making him look smaller than he was.

I opened the door.

"Justin."

I walked through it.

The elevator took a long time to come. I stood in the hallway, staring at the floor numbers above the door — 14, 13, 12, climbing slowly from wherever it had been — and I didn't think. I actively, deliberately, with effort, did not think. I stared at the numbers and I waited and I felt the absence of thought as a physical sensation, like a room that had been emptied of furniture while you were standing in it.

The elevator arrived. I got in. The doors closed. I pressed L for lobby and watched the numbers descend.

I didn't look at my phone. I didn't look at anything. I just stood in the elevator and let it carry me down, floor by floor, away from the fourteenth floor and the apartment and the bed and the ceiling I'd been staring at when everything was calm, when everything was okay, when everything was the opposite of what it turned out to be.

The lobby. The doorman. The night. The cold.

I walked.
