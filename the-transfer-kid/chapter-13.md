# Chapter 13: The Gala

*(POV: Zack)*
I didn't know Andrew Hayes. Not really.

I knew his name the way you know the names of people you pass every day without ever stopping — a label attached to a face, filed under "Westbrook, third floor, usually alone." I'd seen him in hallways. I'd seen him at assemblies, sitting in the back row with his bag on the seat next to him like he was saving it for someone who was never going to show up. I'd heard his name mentioned in the way that Westbrook mentioned anyone who didn't fit: quietly, as a footnote, the kind of person teachers remembered and students didn't.

Andrew was the quiet one. That was his entire identity at Westbrook Prep — or at least, that was all I ever had access to. He wasn't bullied. He wasn't ignored in the aggressive way. He was just absent from the social machinery of the school, moving through it without ever being caught by any of the gears. The kind of student who got perfect grades and ate lunch by the window in the library and left campus every afternoon at 3:05 without talking to anyone.

I never thought much about it. I had my own things going on — swim team, a decent group of friends, parents who showed up to everything and expected me to do the same. Andrew Hayes existed in my peripheral vision the way a lot of people did: technically present, functionally invisible.

If someone had told me, in June of that year, that Andrew Hayes would become the most important person in my life, I would have laughed. Not cruelly — I wasn't that kind of person. Just the honest laugh you give when someone says something that doesn't connect to any reality you can imagine.

June had other plans.

---

The first sound wasn't what I expected.

People talk about gunshots sounding like firecrackers, or like a car backfiring, or like something you might mistake for something else. They didn't. They sounded like gunshots — sharp, percussive, too loud for the room, the kind of sound that bypasses your brain and goes straight to your body. My shoulders went up before I knew what was happening. My hands came off the pillar. My whole system shifted from idle to something I didn't have a word for.

Forty minutes earlier, I'd been in a car.

My mother was driving. She drove the way she did everything — precisely, with both hands on the wheel and her eyes checking the mirrors at intervals that might have been timed. My father was in the passenger seat, adjusting the cuffs of his shirt, pulling the fabric so the links sat at the exact angle he preferred. The radio was on — classical, my mother's choice, something with strings that existed in the car the way wallpaper exists in a room: present, unremarked upon, part of the background architecture of their life together.

I was in the backseat. Suit on, tie done, shoes that I'd polished that morning because my mother had set them outside my bedroom door with a note that said "polish" and nothing else, which was her way of issuing instructions without admitting she was issuing them. The suit fit slightly too well — she'd taken me to get it adjusted two weeks earlier, standing behind the tailor and making small corrections to the fabric with the precision of someone who believed that a well-dressed son reflected a well-managed household.

"You look nice," she'd said when I came downstairs, which from my mother meant: acceptable. Adequate. The version of her son that could be presented to the people she was about to spend the evening with.

"Thanks," I'd said.

"Stand up straighter."

I stood up straighter. This was the rhythm of our household — instructions delivered as observations, compliance performed as courtesy. I didn't resent it. It was just how we worked. My father ran the outside world; my mother ran us.

In the car, she asked me about swim practice. I told her it was fine. She asked about the 200 IM time I'd posted at the last meet. I told her it was two seconds off my PR. She nodded — not disappointed, just recording the data. My father said nothing. He'd watch the meet footage later, on his phone, in his study, with the door half-open so I'd know he was watching without him having to say so.

We pulled into the venue parking lot at 6:40. The building was a renovated event hall — old money architecture, the kind with columns out front that were decorative now but might have been structural once. Cars were already lining up. My mother checked her lipstick in the rearview mirror. My father opened his door and buttoned his jacket in one motion, the automatic gesture of a man who'd been attending events like this since before I was born.

"Two hours," my mother said to me. "Maybe three. We'll leave when they start repeating themselves."

This was our tradition. Every year, the same estimate, the same exit strategy. The gala was a social obligation, not a passion. My parents attended because attending was what people in their position did. They wrote a check, shook hands, ate food they didn't choose, and left before the event became the kind of party that lasted past midnight. Efficient. Controlled. The Chen family way.

---

Then the explosion.

Not in the room — somewhere nearby. Below, maybe, or in the adjacent hall. The floor shook. The chandeliers swayed. One of the large windows on the east side cracked — not shattered, just cracked — and the sound of it was like a branch snapping in a silent forest, somehow louder than it should have been.

The chandeliers. I'd noticed them when we walked in — three of them, large, the kind with actual crystal that caught the light and scattered it across the ceiling in patterns that moved when people walked underneath. My mother had looked up at them and said "those are original," meaning original to the building, meaning old, meaning expensive in a way that had nothing to do with price and everything to do with time. I'd looked at them and thought: heavy. The kind of heavy where if one came down, anyone underneath it would not be having a good evening.

They swayed now. Didn't fall. But the swaying was wrong — too wide, too slow, the motion of something that had been stable for decades suddenly being reminded that stability is a choice, not a state.

Then everything happened at once.

The lights went out. Not all of them — a few emergency strips flickered on near the exits, casting the room in a sick orange glow that made everything look underwater. People screamed. People ran. The organized, polite crowd that had been standing in small groups sipping drinks and discussing their children's college plans turned into something unrecognizable in about three seconds. Chairs went over. Tables shifted. Someone fell near the stage and someone else tripped over them and then there were bodies on the floor and people stepping around them and the air had changed — it smelled wrong, chemical, like something burning that shouldn't be.

I lost my parents in the first thirty seconds.

I don't mean I lost track of them. I mean I was standing near a pillar and then the crowd surged and then I was somewhere else and they were nowhere I could see. I called out — once, maybe twice — but the noise ate my voice. I couldn't hear anything except the collective sound of several hundred people trying to leave a room that had only four exits and at least two of them were blocked by something I couldn't see.

Twenty minutes before the shots, my father had been across the room, drink in hand, talking to a colleague from his firm — a man I'd met twice and whose name I kept forgetting, which my mother found unacceptable. She'd been next to him, half-listening, half-scanning the room the way she always did at these things — cataloguing who was here, who was with whom, who had aged since last year. She was good at this. She could walk into a room of two hundred people and within ten minutes tell you who had gotten divorced, who had a new job, and who was wearing the same dress as last year. Social intelligence deployed as quietly and efficiently as everything else she did.

My father had caught my eye at one point — across the room, over the rim of his glass — and given me a small nod. Not a greeting. An acknowledgment. I'm here, you're here, we're both doing what we're supposed to be doing. The Chen family check-in, executed in a quarter of a second without anyone around us noticing.

Now they were gone. Somewhere in the dark, in the screaming, in the surge of bodies moving toward exits that might or might not be accessible. I didn't know if they were together. I didn't know if they were standing or on the floor or outside or under something that had fallen. I knew nothing except that the pillar I'd been leaning against was behind me and my parents were not in front of me and the room was getting worse.

I moved without thinking. Not toward the exits — they were packed, bodies pressed against doors, the kind of crowd density that kills people through compression. I moved sideways, along the wall, looking for space, looking for air, looking for anything that wasn't the center of the room where the smoke was thickening and the sounds were getting worse.

---

I found Andrew by accident.

The east section of the ballroom had partially collapsed. Not the ceiling — a structural column, one of the decorative ones that also apparently held something up, had come down at an angle. It had taken part of the wall with it, creating a V-shaped gap filled with plaster dust and broken drywall and a section of lighting rig that had dropped from the ceiling.

Andrew was underneath it.

Not buried — not yet. He was on the ground, on his side, with the column resting at an angle across a table that had caught most of the weight. The table was bowing. The column was still settling, small pieces of plaster still falling, and Andrew was in the gap between the column and the floor with maybe eighteen inches of clearance that was getting smaller.

I didn't think about it. I just went.

I got down on my knees next to the gap and reached in. Andrew's face was turned toward me, covered in white dust, and his eyes were open and focused in a way that didn't match the situation. Not panicked. Not frozen. Just awake — completely, impossibly awake, like the adrenaline had burned away everything except the ability to see and process.

"Give me your hand," I said.

He did. His grip was tight. I pulled. The dust made everything slippery, and my knees were sliding on the marble floor, and the column above us made a sound — a deep, structural groan that I felt in my sternum — and I pulled harder.

Andrew came out. Not smoothly — his jacket caught on something and tore, and he hit his shoulder on the edge of the table, and I fell backward and he came with me and we landed in a heap on the marble about four feet from where he'd been.

The column finished falling. The table underneath it gave way with a crack that sounded like a bone breaking, and the whole thing came down flat against the floor where Andrew had been lying three seconds earlier.

We looked at the spot. Then we looked at each other.

The ballroom had been beautiful. That was the thing my brain kept reaching for — the before-image, stubbornly persisting underneath the after. Less than an hour ago, the room had been the kind of space that made you stand differently. High ceilings. The three chandeliers. Round tables with white cloths and centerpieces that someone had spent hours arranging — flowers and candles in glass holders that caught the light and threw it sideways. A small stage at one end where someone would eventually make a speech about children's literacy or pediatric funding or whichever cause was being featured this year. Wait staff in black, moving between groups with trays of things I couldn't identify — small, carefully constructed, the kind of food that exists to be looked at before it's eaten.

The room I'd walked into was gone. The room I was sitting in now was made of dust and emergency lighting and the sound of a building deciding what shape it wanted to be next.

His face was still covered in dust. Mine probably was too. We were both breathing like we'd just run a mile, and the smoke was getting thicker, and somewhere in the building another sound happened — not an explosion, something smaller, structural — and the floor vibrated under my palms.

"We need to move," I said.

We moved. Or tried to. I got about five steps before I realized something was wrong with my left leg — not pain, exactly, but wrongness. A feeling of misalignment, like a hinge that had been bent past its range. I looked down and then immediately looked away, because whatever was happening below my knee was not something I wanted to process while we were still inside a building that was actively getting worse.

Andrew saw me stop. He came back. Put my arm over his shoulders without asking. We moved together, slower now, toward the nearest exit.

The exit was blocked. A section of the ceiling had come down across it — not enough to seal it completely, but enough that climbing through would require strength and balance that neither of us had. The next exit was across the room, which was now mostly dark and filled with smoke that sat in layers, thicker near the ceiling, almost breathable near the floor.

We weren't getting out.

Andrew guided us to a corner where the air was marginally better — near a ventilation grate in the wall that was still pulling slightly. We sat down with our backs against the wall, shoulder to shoulder, and the noise of the building settled around us like weather.

---

"My parents," Andrew said. "They were — I need to find them."

"We can't move," I said. "The exits are blocked."

"They were near the stage. They —"

"I know. I saw them."

He looked at me. The dust on his face was streaked where sweat had run through it, and his eyes were the only clean part of him — dark brown, steady, unblinking.

"You saw them?"

"Before. They were near a table on the left side. The man and the woman."

"My parents."

"Yeah."

I'd seen more than I told him.

I'd been standing near one of the pillars — existing politely, waiting for the evening to be over, the way I existed at every one of these events — when I first noticed Andrew across the room. Maybe forty feet away, standing near the edge of a table with two adults. A man and a woman. The man was tall, dark-haired, with the kind of posture that comes from either military training or expensive schools. He was leaning toward the woman slightly, saying something with a half-smile that looked private — the kind of thing you say to someone you've been married to long enough that your jokes don't need context. The woman was shorter, composed, holding a glass of wine with both hands and laughing at something the man had said. Not the public laugh — the real one. The one that comes before you remember other people can see you.

They looked like they were having a good evening. They looked like people who had done this a hundred times and would do it a hundred more — the annual gala, the suits, the dresses, the routine of showing up and being seen and going home together in the same car. They looked permanent. The kind of couple you see and think: those two are going to be fine.

Andrew had been standing slightly apart from them, hands in his pockets, watching the room. Not uncomfortable. Not bored. Just present, in the way he was always present at Westbrook. His mother had turned to him at one point and said something — I was too far away to hear, but I saw her hand touch his arm, briefly, the casual contact of a parent who touches their child without thinking about it. Andrew hadn't flinched. Hadn't reacted at all, really. Just nodded at whatever she'd said and gone back to watching the room.

I'd looked at them for maybe ten seconds. A family. A normal family at a normal event. The father with his private joke. The mother with her real laugh. The son who stood apart but stayed close.

I'd gone back to waiting for my parents to decide it was time to leave. That was the last time I saw Andrew's parents standing.

---

He didn't say anything for a few seconds. Then he pulled his phone out of his pocket — cracked screen, but still lit — and dialed. I watched his face while it rang. The orange emergency lights caught the dust in the air between us, turning everything into a slow, thick haze.

Someone picked up. Andrew's whole body shifted — not relaxed, just changed, like a machine switching states.

"Ethan." His voice was controlled. Flat. The voice of someone reporting a situation. "I'm in the east side. Near the back wall. I can't get to the exits."

A pause. I could hear the other voice — not the words, but the texture. Low. Fast. Also controlled, but in a different way. The way someone sounds when they're managing information that's coming in from multiple directions and none of it is good.

Andrew asked: "Are Mom and Dad okay?"

The silence on the other end lasted about two seconds. Then the brother's voice came back. I couldn't make out the words, but the cadence was even. Measured. The kind of delivery you use when you've already decided what to say and how to say it before the question was asked.

Andrew exhaled. The kind of exhale that comes from a place deeper than your lungs — the kind that releases something you've been holding without knowing you were holding it.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. Yeah. I'll wait."

He hung up. Sat there for a moment, staring at his phone.

"They're fine," he said. To me. Like I'd asked. "My brother says they got out. They're fine."

I said nothing.

I didn't say anything because I didn't know anything — not for certain. I hadn't seen his parents leave. I'd seen them standing near a table before the first shots — the man with his posture and his half-smile, the woman with her wine glass and her real laugh — and then I'd seen nothing except smoke and bodies and the inside of a collapsed wall.

But I also said nothing because of the brother's voice. The controlled cadence. The two-second pause before answering. I'm seventeen, not stupid. I know what it sounds like when someone is deciding in real time how much truth a person can handle. I know what it sounds like when someone is managing terrible information and choosing, deliberately, to distribute it in pieces.

The brother had said they were fine.

Andrew believed him.

I thought about the woman's hand on Andrew's arm. The casual, automatic touch. The kind of contact that only exists between people who are permanent to each other — parents and children, the foundational relationship, the one you build everything else on top of. I thought about the man's half-smile, directed at his wife, private and easy, the smile of someone who assumed there would be a thousand more evenings to smile at her across.

I sat next to Andrew in the dark, with my leg wrong and the air getting worse and the sounds of the building settling around us like an animal going to sleep, and I let him believe it. Because right now, in this corner, with no way out and nothing to do but wait, belief was the only thing either of us had.

My mother was out there somewhere. My father was out there somewhere. Maybe together, maybe not. Maybe already outside, maybe pressed against a blocked exit, maybe on the floor. My mother, who set polished shoes outside my bedroom door and called it a note. My father, who checked in with a quarter-second nod across a crowded room and called it love.

I didn't look at my leg. I didn't think about my parents. I just sat there, shoulder to shoulder with a kid I'd never spoken to, and breathed.
