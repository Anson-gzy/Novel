# Chapter 9: Invisible

*(POV: Marcus)*
Here's something I learned about Andrew Hayes over the course of November: he knows exactly who I am.

We share a class. We've talked — not deep, not personal, but enough. He nodded at me the first day he walked into Government. He's answered questions I've asked. He borrowed a pen from me once, gave it back the next day, said thanks. There is no version of reality where Andrew Hayes doesn't know my name, doesn't recognize my face, doesn't have me filed away somewhere in the mental system he uses to keep track of the people around him.

But in the hallway — in any space that isn't a classroom with a seating chart and a teacher keeping track — Andrew looks straight through me.

Not past me. Through me. Like I'm part of the architecture. Like I'm a locker or a fire extinguisher or one of those motivational posters nobody reads. His eyes sweep the hallway the way anyone's do — checking the current, noting who's where — and when they cross the space where I'm standing, they don't stop. They don't slow. They don't register anything at all.

It's not hostile. That's the part that got me. If it were hostile — if he looked at me and then looked away on purpose, if there was a flicker of something before the blankness — I'd understand it. That's a choice. That's a person deciding they don't want to deal with you. I can work with that.

This is different. This is absence. This is someone who has decided, at some level below conscious thought, that I am not a person who exists in hallway-space. I exist in Government class and nowhere else. The rest of the time, I'm furniture.

I am not someone who lets things bother him quietly. I say what I think. I deal with things directly. If you annoy me, you'll know about it. If you confuse me, I'll ask until I'm not confused anymore.

But this one got under my skin in a way I couldn't immediately explain, and the fact that I couldn't explain it made it worse.

---

I tested it. I'm not proud of that — testing implies caring, and I hadn't decided yet whether I cared. But I did it anyway.

First time: I walked past Andrew's locker between third and fourth period. He was standing there, shoving a textbook into the top shelf, and I said, "Hey," as I passed. Not loud. Not aggressive. Just the basic thing you say to someone you know.

He glanced up. Half a second of eye contact — just enough for me to confirm that yes, he saw me, yes, his brain processed the input — and then nothing. Eyes went back to the locker. Hands kept moving. No response. Not even a nod.

I kept walking. I told myself it didn't mean anything.

Second time: Government class, the following week. Okoro had us doing small-group discussion on executive overreach. Andrew was in my cluster — four desks pushed together, three other people I barely remembered, and Andrew sitting across from me with his notebook open. I made a point about term limits. Not a throwaway line — an actual argument, the kind I'd thought about. Andrew looked at me while I said it, and when I finished, he responded. Directly. Functionally. He agreed with part of it, pushed back on another part, cited something from the reading I hadn't caught. It was a real exchange — three minutes of back-and-forth where he treated me like a person who had things worth saying.

Then the discussion ended. Okoro moved on. Andrew turned back to his notebook. And the wall came down like someone had flipped a switch. He didn't look at me again for the rest of the period. Not once. I was sitting three feet away and I had stopped existing.

I packed up my things at the bell and walked out. I felt something that was close to being insulted, though I wouldn't have used that word if someone asked.

---

I brought it up with Dylan first. We were walking to the bus stop — me, Dylan, and Tyler — and I kept it casual. An observation. Nothing loaded.

"Andrew does this thing," I said. "Where he talks to you in class like you're a real person and then passes you in the hallway like you're invisible."

"Maybe he's just not a hallway person," Dylan said.

"What does that mean?"

"Some people are different in different settings. Like how you're nicer when you're tired."

"I'm not nicer when I'm tired."

"You are. You lose the edge. It's almost pleasant."

Tyler looked at me. "Is he doing it to you specifically?"

"I think so."

"That's weird," Tyler said, and then immediately moved on to something about a video he'd watched, which was Tyler's way of telling me he didn't have more to offer on the subject.

Dylan made another joke — something about Andrew having a "talk to" list and a "walk past" list and me being on the wrong one. I smiled because it was funny, but it didn't answer anything.

I mentioned it to Samira the next day. We were in the library during free period — she was reading something on her phone, I was pretending to work on an essay — and I said it differently this time. Less like an observation, more like a question.

"Have you noticed that Andrew is weird with certain people?"

Samira looked up. "Weird how?"

"Cold. Not mean — just blank. Like they don't register."

She thought about it for a second. "Maybe he's just like that with some people. Some people have a limit on how many relationships they maintain at once. The rest just don't make the cut."

"He's not like that with everyone, though. I've watched him be warm with plenty of people. He was warm with Justin for weeks. He's warm with half the people in the hallway — you can see it, the way he turns it on."

Samira set her phone down. "So he's warm with some people and not with you."

"That's what I'm saying."

"And that bothers you."

I paused. "I didn't say it bothered me."

"You're bringing it up in the library on a Tuesday. It bothers you."

She had me there. I didn't respond, and Samira, being Samira, didn't push. She just picked her phone back up and went back to whatever she was reading, and the silence settled between us like she'd said exactly what she needed to say and nothing more.

But I kept thinking about it. She'd offered a clean explanation — maybe Andrew is just like that with some people — and I didn't find it satisfying. Because I'd watched him. I'd been watching him for weeks, in Government and in the hallways and in the small moments between classes where people showed you who they really were. Andrew Hayes could be warm. He could be magnetic. He could make you feel like the most important person in the room just by tilting his head and listening.

He did none of that with me. Not because he couldn't. Because he chose not to.

That difference mattered, and I couldn't stop picking at it.

---

Eventually I said something to Justin.

We were at lunch — the full table, everyone accounted for. The conversation was moving in its usual rhythm: Dylan running commentary, Becca scrolling, Tyler saying something accidentally sharp that nobody fully acknowledged. I waited for a gap and then dropped it in, the way you toss something into a conversation and pretend you don't care where it lands.

"Hey, has anyone else noticed that Andrew kind of ignores certain people?"

Dylan glanced at me. "You're still on this?"

"I'm not on anything. I'm asking a question."

"You mentioned this last week."

"And now I'm mentioning it again. Different audience." I looked at Justin. "You know him better than any of us. Does he do that? Go completely blank with people he's already met?"

Justin was holding his fork halfway to his mouth. He put it down.

"I don't know," he said. "I mean — Andrew's quiet. That's just how he is. He doesn't talk to a lot of people."

"He talks to plenty of people. I've seen it."

"Okay, but that doesn't mean he's ignoring you specifically. Maybe he's just — busy. Or distracted. Or not thinking about it."

"He's thinking about everything. That's the thing about him. Everything he does is calculated."

Justin's expression shifted. Not angry — something more reflexive than that. Defensive in a way that happened before the thought caught up. "That's not fair. You don't know what's going on with him. He could have a lot on his mind. His parents died, Marcus. Maybe he's not spending a lot of energy on hallway greetings."

The table went quiet. Not the fun kind of quiet — the kind where everyone suddenly has something very interesting on their phone or their tray.

I looked at Justin. He looked back at me. There was something in his face that I recognized — the specific expression of someone who'd just said more than they intended to and was now trying to figure out how to walk it back without admitting they needed to.

"I didn't say he was doing anything wrong," I said. "I said he ignores me. That's an observation."

"It sounded like a complaint."

"It wasn't."

"Then what was it?"

I shrugged. "Just wondering if anyone else noticed."

Dylan cleared his throat. "Wow. Okay. So we're doing this. Justin is defending Andrew's honor at the lunch table. That's where we are now."

"I'm not defending his honor —"

"You kind of are," Becca said, gently.

"I'm just saying Marcus is reading into something that's probably not there."

"That's what defending means," Tyler said, without looking up from his chips.

The teasing started after that — light, the way it always was with us. Dylan made a joke about Justin being Andrew's unofficial PR team. Becca asked if Justin wanted to be Andrew's spokesperson and suggested business cards. Tyler said something about how Justin should start charging Andrew a retainer. Justin took it in stride, laughed it off, told everyone to shut up, and the conversation moved on.

Nobody was actually fighting. The table went back to normal inside of two minutes.

But I filed it away. The speed of it — the way Justin had jumped in before his brain caught up. The instinct. He hadn't thought about defending Andrew. He'd just done it. Automatically. The way you defend someone who matters to you, someone you've decided is worth protecting, even from a comment that wasn't really an attack.

Justin had said, weeks ago, that he barely knew Andrew. That it was casual. That they'd hung out once and it was fine.

The way he'd just gone to bat told a different story. And I didn't think Justin knew it yet, which made it worse.

I ate my lunch. I let the conversation carry me. I didn't bring it up again.

But I was still watching. And now I was watching two things at once.
