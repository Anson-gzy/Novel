# Leviticus: Strange Fire — Global Notes

## One: Writing Style

- **Australian English.** Colour, favour, recognise. Slang: mate, reckon, arsehole, c**t (used casually, not always as insult), nah, yeah nah, heaps, servo, tinny.
- **Internal monologue driven.** Naim thinks in fragments. He doesn't analyse, he notices. Show the noticing; let the reader do the analysis.
- **Short sentences for tension.** Long sentences for the quiet moments. The horror sections should feel breathless.
- **No literary over-writing.** This is a teen who didn't finish his ATAR. He doesn't think in metaphors. When a metaphor appears, it should feel accidental, discovered, not crafted.
- **Reference work style cues (from The Transfer Kid):** Slow emotional buildup. Let silences do the work. The strongest moments are the ones where nobody says the important thing.
- **Profanity is natural.** These are Australian teens. F-bombs are punctuation, not emphasis. Don't clean up the dialogue.
- **Horror as pressure, not spectacle.** The entity is terrifying because of what it means, not what it does. The violence is brief and disorienting. The real horror is the dread between encounters.

## Two: Character Reminders

- **Naim:** He apologises too much. He freezes when overwhelmed. He's braver in action than in words. He does NOT over-explain his feelings; he feels them and acts on them or doesn't. He is not articulate about his queerness; it just is. No coming-out speeches.
- **Ryan:** He swears constantly. He's physically expressive (punches walls, grabs Naim's arm, kicks things). He protects by pushing away. When he's genuinely scared, he goes very quiet, and that's when Naim knows it's bad. He's funnier than he gets credit for.
- **Arlene:** She's not a monster. She made a terrible choice out of love and fear. She speaks in deflections and practical things (food, logistics) when she can't handle emotion. "They have those sausage rolls. The ones you like."
- **The entity:** It looks like the person you're most attracted to. It comes when you're alone. It's getting better at looking and acting like the real person. It can be repelled by fire (the lighter). It doesn't speak in the film. In the sequel, it starts speaking.

## Three: Plot and World Prohibitions

- No supernatural explanation beyond what the film establishes. The entity is ambiguously real. It might be psychological. It might be genuinely supernatural. Never resolve this.
- No villain monologues. The healer is pathetic, not grandiose.
- No redemption arc for the community. The town stays complicit. Individual people (Arlene, Rod) can change; the system doesn't.
- No cure. The entity doesn't disappear. The ending is about living with it, not defeating it.
- Hunter's death is never shown in detail. It's referenced, grieved, felt. Never spectacle.
- No homophobic slurs used by the narrative voice. Characters may use coded language ("people like you"), but the text itself never participates in the violence.

## Four: De-AI Rules

**Universal:**
1. No em dashes
2. No rule of three stacking (max 2 per chapter)
3. No copula avoidance (serves as / stands as)
4. No promotional language (vibrant / tapestry / testament / pivotal / crucial)
5. Dialogue: no complete expressions. Real teens interrupt, trail off, change topic mid-sentence
6. No staccato drama (3+ consecutive short sentences for fake tension)
7. Vary sentence length. Never 3 consecutive sentences of similar length.

**English-specific:**
8. No AI vocabulary (delve / underscore / showcase / landscape / interplay / intricate / foster / garner)
9. No elegant variation (don't call Naim "the teen," "the boy," "the young man" in the same paragraph)
10. No false ranges (from X to Y where X and Y aren't on the same axis)
11. No negative parallelisms (Not only...but... / It's not just...it's...)
