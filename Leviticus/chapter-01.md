# Chapter 1: Keep a Place

The bus smelled like someone had cleaned it with something that was supposed to smell like pine but didn't. Naim sat with his knees pressed against the seat in front and his hands between his thighs to stop them shaking. The lighter was in his right pocket. He could feel the shape of it through his jeans.

Three rows back, a woman was sleeping with her mouth open and a canvas bag clutched to her chest. Two seats ahead, a man in a high-vis vest was watching something on his phone with no headphones, the tinny audio bleeding into the silence. A couple near the front were talking in low voices, their heads tilted toward each other.

People. Real people. That was the test.

The entity couldn't appear when other people were around. Jessica had said so. Izzie's brother had said so. If Ryan was here, on this bus, three seats away with his head against the window and his eyes closed and his chest moving in that slow regular way, then he was Ryan. The real one. Because the woman three rows back could see him, and the man in the high-vis could see him, and if Naim asked them "do you see that guy over there?" they'd say yes.

He didn't ask.

Ryan's phone was on the seat between them, face-down, screen cracked from something Naim didn't want to think about. The battery icon had been red for an hour. Naim watched Ryan's reflection in the window, doubled and transparent, floating over the dark paddocks outside. His mouth was slightly open. He looked younger when he slept. Less like someone who'd just driven a car into three people and more like someone who hadn't slept in a week, which he probably hadn't.

Naim picked at the skin around his thumbnail until it stung.

The bus hit a pothole and Ryan's head knocked against the glass. He didn't wake up.

That was the thing Naim kept coming back to. If the entity looked like Ryan, it would also sleep like Ryan. It would also breathe like Ryan. It would know exactly how Ryan's head fell to one side when he was out cold, because it was built from the same source material. Whatever the ritual had done, it had taken a photograph of the person you wanted most and made it walk and talk and touch.

So the fact that Ryan looked real didn't mean he was real.

But the woman three rows back could see him. That was the rule. Naim held onto it the way his mother held onto scripture.

The lighter was warm from his body heat. He touched it through the denim, pressing his thumb against the ridged wheel.

Outside, the paddocks gave way to warehouses, then to houses with lights on, then to wider roads with orange streetlights making the bus interior glow and dim in regular intervals. The city was pulling them in. Naim had been here once, on a school trip to the art gallery. He remembered nothing about the art and everything about the gift shop, where he'd bought a postcard of a painting he thought was beautiful and then thrown it away at the train station because he couldn't explain to his mother why he'd chosen a nude.

That felt like a hundred years ago.

Ryan stirred. Opened his eyes, blinked at the window like he'd forgotten where he was, then looked at Naim.

"How long?"

"About twenty minutes, I reckon."

Ryan sat up and rubbed the back of his neck. His knuckles were swollen and dark, the skin split over the second and third fingers. He noticed Naim looking and curled his hand into his lap.

"You been awake the whole time?"

"Yeah."

"You should've slept."

"Nah."

Ryan looked at him. Really looked, in that way he had where his whole face went still and his eyes did something Naim couldn't describe. Not soft, exactly. More like he was trying to memorise something he thought he might lose.

"It's me," Ryan said.

"I know."

"You've been staring at me for four hours."

"I wasn't staring."

"Naim."

"I was just checking."

Ryan exhaled through his nose. Almost a laugh. "Checking what?"

"That you're. You know."

"Real?"

Naim didn't answer. He pulled the elastic band on his left wrist, stretched it an inch, let it snap back. The sting was small and specific and it grounded him for about half a second.

"Where'd you get that?" Ryan asked.

"I don't know. Hospital, maybe."

Ryan watched him snap it again, twice.

"Stop that."

"It helps."

"It doesn't help. You're just hurting yourself and calling it something else."

Naim stopped. He tucked the elastic band under his sleeve.

The bus was slowing. The pneumatic brakes hissed and the driver said something over the intercom that was too garbled to understand. Lights. A station. People standing on the platform in the flat blue glow of the shelters, looking at their phones or staring at nothing.

"This is it?" Naim asked.

"Nah. This is Strathfield. Central's another twenty." Ryan leaned forward and looked out the window. "You been to the city before?"

"Once."

"Right. Do you know anyone here?"

"No."

"Yeah. Me neither."

The doors opened. A few people got on. The woman three rows back woke up, gathered her canvas bag, and shuffled off. Naim watched her go and felt something cold settle in his stomach. One fewer witness.

The new passengers spread through the bus. A girl with massive headphones. An older bloke reading a newspaper, which Naim hadn't seen someone do in years. A woman carrying grocery bags that crinkled with every step.

The doors closed. The bus pulled away.

"We need somewhere to sleep," Naim said.

"I've got, like, sixty bucks."

"That's not enough for a room."

"I know that."

"So what do we do?"

Ryan was quiet for a while. He picked up his phone, pressed the power button. The screen stayed black.

"There's Maccas near Central," he said. "Open all night. We can sit there till morning, figure it out."

"We can't sleep at Maccas."

"We're not gonna sleep, Naim."

Naim looked at him.

"What, you were gonna close your eyes?" Ryan said. "Tonight? After everything?"

He had a point. Naim's body was running on something that wasn't adrenaline anymore, something slower and more corrosive that made his teeth ache and his eyes feel like they'd been peeled. Sleep wasn't coming. Not tonight. Probably not tomorrow either.

"What about the car?" Naim asked.

"What about it?"

"Where'd you leave it?"

"Bus station car park. Back home."

"That's Izzie's parents' car."

"Yep."

"They'll find it."

"Yep."

"And then they'll know we left."

Ryan turned to face Naim fully. His eyes were bloodshot and the left side of his jaw had a bruise that was already going purple-green.

"They already know we left."

The bus reached Central Station at twenty past midnight. The city at night was nothing like the city Naim remembered from the school trip. It was louder and emptier at the same time, full of sound that came from nowhere specific. Trains. Ventilation. A siren blocks away, rising and falling.

They walked through the station concourse, which was mostly deserted except for a couple of transit cops standing near the turnstiles, and out through the south exit onto Eddy Avenue. The air was warm and thick, the kind of Sydney summer heat that didn't break after dark.

Naim realised he didn't have a bag. He'd left the house with nothing but his phone, his lighter, and whatever was in his pockets when his mother drove him to the healer. Ryan had a backpack that he'd grabbed from the car, slung over one shoulder.

"What's in there?"

"Clothes. Charger. Nothing useful."

They found the McDonald's on George Street. It was the brightest thing on the block, a box of fluorescent light in a row of closed shopfronts. Inside, a security guard sat near the door scrolling his phone. A couple of delivery drivers waited by the counter. A man in a suit was eating a Quarter Pounder with the focused determination of someone who'd been drinking.

Ryan bought two large Cokes and a twenty-pack of nuggets. They sat in a booth near the back, under a light that buzzed at a frequency Naim could feel in his fillings.

"You hungry?" Ryan pushed the nuggets toward him.

"Not really."

"Eat anyway."

Naim ate three nuggets. They tasted like nothing. He drank the Coke and the sugar hit his bloodstream like a small electric shock.

Ryan ate mechanically, looking at the door every time it opened. The security guard. The delivery drivers. The suited man finishing his burger. Witnesses. All of them.

"We have to sleep eventually," Naim said.

"I know."

"We can't both be awake all the time."

"I know, Naim."

"So one of us sleeps and the other watches."

Ryan looked at him. Chewed. Swallowed.

"And what does the one watching look for?"

The question sat between them like something with weight. Naim put down his Coke. The fluorescent light buzzed.

What does the one watching look for? The answer was: you. You look for the second version of me, or I look for the second version of you. You look for the thing that wears my face and moves like me and knows exactly how I hold a chip or scratch my elbow or breathe when I'm sleeping. And if you see two of me, one of them isn't me, and you have to figure out which one fast enough to not die.

"I'll take first shift," Ryan said. "You sleep. When it gets light, we swap."

"Ryan."

"What?"

"How will you know it's me? When I wake up."

Ryan picked up a nugget, bit into it, looked at the table.

"I'll know."

"But how?"

"Because the thing, whatever it is, it doesn't know everything. It looks right and it sounds right but it gets the small shit wrong. Jessica said that. The girl at the hospital."

"She said it gets better."

"Then we'll deal with that when it gets better."

Ryan finished the nugget. Wiped his fingers on his jeans. Leaned back in the booth and stretched his arms along the back of the seat.

"Get some sleep, Naim."

Naim folded his arms on the table and rested his head on them. The Formica was cool against his forehead. He could hear the buzzing light, the delivery driver's phone ringing, the distant hiss of the soft-drink machine. He could hear Ryan breathing.

He closed his eyes.

And behind his eyelids, the thing he kept trying not to think about assembled itself in the dark: the mill, the screaming, the lighter flame, Ryan's body slamming into the wall. The entity wearing Ryan's face. The sound it made when the fire touched it, that scream that didn't come from a throat.

His mother in the car, saying: "We need fear, Naim. It's how we survive."

He opened his eyes. Ryan was watching the door.

"Ryan."

"Yeah."

"When you said 'don't go.' On the bus, before. Back home."

"Yeah."

"Did you mean it?"

Ryan looked down at him. Naim's face pressed against his folded arms, one eye open, the elastic band visible where his sleeve had ridden up.

"Yeah," Ryan said. "I meant it."

Naim closed his eyes again.

He didn't sleep. But he stopped watching for a while, and that was close enough.
